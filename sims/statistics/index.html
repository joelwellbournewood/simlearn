What am I looking at?

Five machines, each driven by nothing but chance, and each producing something you can predict in advance. One coin flip is beyond anybody's power to forecast. A million coin flips are so predictable that insurers, pollsters, drug regulators, and particle physicists stake their livelihoods on the answer. Everything here lives on the road between those two facts.

Pick a machine on the left. In every one, the red dashed line is a prediction made from the settings alone, before any data lands, and the coloured shapes are what the machine actually did. Watching the two meet is the whole exercise.

  • Galton board. Balls fall through rows of pegs, going left or right at each one with no memory of the last. Drag the funnel sideways to move where the bell forms, or downwards to take rows away, and the prediction re-forms at the new starting point while the bins beneath stay where they are.
  • Population sampling. Draw one value at a time from height, wealth, finch beaks, asteroid diameters, lottery balls, or a coin, average each handful, and drop the average into a histogram. At one draw per sample you are looking at the population itself. Raise the sample size and every one of those populations, however lopsided or spiky, produces the same bell.
  • Confidence intervals. One fixed truth, measured over and over by small samples, each measurement wrapped in an interval. Green intervals caught the truth, red ones missed, and the tally at the bottom is the only thing entitled to the words 95 percent.
  • Screening test. A blood marker overlaps between the healthy and the ill. You choose where to call a result positive, and the two ways of being wrong, a false alarm and a missed case, trade against each other with every step of the threshold. The table fills with real people as they are screened.
  • Monte Carlo darts. Darts thrown blindly at a square of side 2, with a circle of radius 1 inside it, measure the area of that circle. Count the fraction that land in the circle, multiply by four, and you have π out of pure noise.

Things to try

  • On the Galton board, drag the funnel to the far edge. Balls that fall off the board are counted separately and the bell you can see is a truncated one, which is what happens whenever a measuring instrument runs out of range.
  • Still on the board, drag the funnel to the floor. Two rows of pegs give three bins and no bell at all, and the bell grows back one row at a time as you lift it.
  • In population sampling, choose Wealth and leave draws per sample at 1, then raise it slowly. The lopsidedness survives to about eight draws, and is gone by thirty. Now try Asteroids, whose tail is much heavier, and notice that thirty draws are no longer enough.
  • In population sampling, choose Coin with 2 draws per sample. Three outcomes, in a 1:2:1 ratio, and the red prediction has three steps because the exact answer has three steps.
  • In confidence intervals, drop the sample size to 3 and switch between the two critical values. Both hold near 95 percent when they are used honestly, and the z intervals all have the same length while the t intervals vary wildly, because each one is guessing the spread from three numbers.
  • In the screening test, set the disease to 1 in 1000 and the test to 3 sd apart, which is a good test. Most people who test positive still do not have the disease. Then find the threshold that makes the false alarms and the missed cases roughly equal, and see what it costs.

Why pegs make a bell

Each peg gives a ball one nudge, left or right, independent of every nudge before it. After n rows the landing column is fixed by the number of rightward nudges, so the column counts follow the binomial distribution B(n, p) exactly. De Moivre showed in 1733 that this distribution approaches the normal curve as n grows, and the board turns his theorem into an object. The dashed curve is

mean μ = origin + n(p − ½), spread σ = √(np(1−p))

in units of bin columns, computed from the funnel position and the sliders alone. Moving the funnel starts a fresh count at the new position, because the old balls were produced by a different machine and pooling them would describe neither. The bins themselves never move, so the two piles can be compared directly.

What the central limit theorem does and does not say

The theorem says that the mean of n independent draws from a population with mean μ and finite standard deviation σ has a distribution that approaches

x̄ ∼ N(μ, σ/√n)

as n grows, whatever the shape of the population. Three details do the real work. The approach is asymptotic, so at small n the answer depends on the population, which is why the red line here is the exact distribution of the mean rather than the normal approximation: it is the population's own distribution convolved with itself n times, computed numerically, and at n = 1 and n = 2 you can see it disagree with any bell. The rate of approach depends on how skewed and how heavy tailed the population is, so the familiar rule of thumb that thirty is enough is a summary of the easy cases, not a theorem. And the width shrinks like 1/√n, so halving your error costs four times the sample, which is the arithmetic behind the price of every poll and every clinical trial.

The populations offered here are the standard cast, in the sense that each is a real quantity known to take that shape: adult height is close to normal, household wealth is lognormal with a long right tail, the bills of the black-bellied seedcracker, an African finch, come in a large form and a small one with almost nothing in between, plus a rare third that is larger still, asteroid diameters follow a power law with countless small bodies and a few enormous ones, and lottery balls are as flat as anything gets.

What 95 percent confidence promises, and to whom

An interval built from a sample of n measurements is

x̄ ± c · (spread) / √n

Neyman's 1937 formulation, the one this machine animates, makes a promise about the procedure: run it many times and 95 percent of the intervals it produces will contain the truth. The single interval in your hand either contains the truth or does not, and no probability attaches to it after the fact. That is why the tally at the bottom of the screen is the honest way to state the guarantee, and it is also the reason the phrase is so often misreported.

Why the choice between z and t exists

The interval needs the spread of the population, and there are two situations.

When σ is genuinely known, from a long calibration history of the instrument or from a physical argument, the standardised error follows a normal distribution and the critical value comes from it: 1.96 for 95 percent, whatever n is. Every interval then has the same length, since only the sample mean changes from experiment to experiment.

When σ is unknown, which is the usual case, it has to be estimated from the same small sample that produced the mean. Two quantities are now uncertain instead of one, and the standardised error follows Student's t distribution with ν = n − 1 degrees of freedom, which has heavier tails than the normal. The critical value is larger, and it grows as the sample shrinks: 2.26 at n = 10, and 4.30 at n = 3, against 1.96 for z. Gosset worked this out in 1908 while checking small batches at the Guinness brewery, and published as "Student" because his employer treated the statistics as a trade secret.

Both settings here are implemented honestly, so both hold near their nominal coverage and you can compare them fairly. The classic mistake, which this machine deliberately does not commit, is to use the sample's own spread with the z value of 1.96, as if the estimate were the truth. At n = 3 that produces intervals roughly half the honest length, and coverage sinks to about 80 percent while the label still claims 95.

Two ways to be wrong, and why the base rate decides

The screening machine draws the two things a test can get wrong. A type I error is a false alarm, a healthy person called positive, at rate α. A type II error is a missed case, an ill person called negative, at rate β. Sensitivity is 1 − β, the share of ill people the test catches. Specificity is 1 − α, the share of healthy people it clears. Moving the threshold moves both, always in opposite directions, and the pair of them traces out the ROC curve used to compare tests in medicine, radar, and machine learning. Neyman and Pearson framed the trade-off in 1933, and it is the reason a test can be described honestly only by two numbers rather than one.

The number a patient actually cares about is a third one. Given a positive result, the chance of really being ill is

P(ill | positive) = prevalence · sensitivity / [prevalence · sensitivity + (1 − prevalence)(1 − specificity)]

which is Bayes' theorem, and it depends on how common the disease is. A test with 99 percent sensitivity and 99 percent specificity, applied to a disease that one person in ten thousand has, produces about a hundred false alarms for every true case, so a positive result means roughly a one percent chance of illness. This is the base rate fallacy, it is measurable in how doctors and patients read screening results, and it is the reason mass screening programmes are argued over rather than simply switched on.

Randomness as an instrument

The dart board estimates π as four times the fraction of uniform darts landing inside the circle, because the circle of radius 1 occupies π/4 of the square of side 2. The error shrinks as 1/√N, slowly, and that slowness is the honest price on every Monte Carlo method. The technique earns its keep where the alternatives are worse: integrals over hundreds of dimensions, neutron transport, financial risk, and the lighting in animated films are all computed by throwing darts, because in high dimensions a random sample beats any grid you could afford to build.

How this one is computed

  • Randomness comes from the browser's pseudo-random generator, with normal draws made by the Box-Muller transform. Nothing is staged, so runs differ every time, and a slow start on the coverage tally is chance doing what chance does.
  • Galton balls decide left or right once per row, which makes the column counts exactly binomial. The bounce you watch is animation layered over that decision, so the physics on screen never disturbs the arithmetic being checked.
  • Each population is stored as a 257 point grid, the sampler draws from that grid, and the red prediction is the exact n-fold convolution of the same grid, coarsened as n grows to keep it fast. Prediction and machine therefore agree by construction at every sample size, including 1 and 2.
  • Student's t critical values come from a series approximation in 1/ν accurate to a few parts in a thousand for ν ≥ 2, the normal quantile from a standard rational approximation, and the normal integral in the screening test from Abramowitz and Stegun 26.2.17.
  • Every overlay is computed from the settings before the data lands, never fitted to the data afterwards, because the point is prediction.

Limits of the model

Everything here assumes draws that are independent and identically distributed, and most statistical disasters in the wild are failures of that assumption rather than of arithmetic. Survey respondents cluster, financial returns move together in a crash, and one contaminated batch correlates a thousand measurements, so real errors shrink more slowly than √n promises, and sometimes barely shrink at all. The central limit theorem also requires a finite variance, and heavy tailed quantities such as catastrophe insurance losses keep their skew long past the folklore sample size of thirty, which the Asteroids population is here to show. A confidence interval guards against sampling luck alone, and no sample size repairs a biased instrument or a leading question. The screening machine assumes two clean normal populations and a test result that means the same thing in everybody, where real markers drift with age, sex, and laboratory. And the disease status here is known, which is exactly what a real screening programme lacks, so its false negatives are visible on screen and invisible in a clinic.
