What am I looking at?

An optical bench seen from the side. A coral arrow stands in front of a lens, light leaves the tip of that arrow in every direction, and the lens bends the rays that reach it so they meet again at one point. Where they meet is the image, and the pale bar on the right is a sensor placed to catch it.

The rays here are traced one by one. Nothing is drawn from a formula for where the image should be; the image appears where the rays actually cross.

The thin lens equation

For a lens thin enough to ignore its own width, the object distance u, the image distance v and the focal length f are related by 1/v = 1/f - 1/u. Magnification is m = -v/u, and the minus sign is why a real image is upside down. The inset marked SENSOR shows the picture the right way round for the sensor, so a negative magnification appears there as an inverted arrow.

Two distances on the axis are marked F and 2F. They are the whole story in miniature. Beyond 2F the image is real, inverted and smaller. At exactly 2F it is real, inverted and the same size. Between F and 2F it is real, inverted and larger. Inside F there is no real image at all.

Real and virtual images

A real image is a place where light genuinely arrives, so a sensor or a sheet of paper put there records it. A virtual image is where the diverging rays appear to come from when your eye traces them backwards, drawn here as dashed lines. Nothing arrives there and no sensor can record it. Press Magnifier to put the object inside the focal length and watch the outgoing rays spread instead of converging.

Aperture, blur and depth of field

When the sensor is not at the image plane, each point of the object lands as a disc rather than a point. That disc is the blur circle, and its diameter is the aperture diameter multiplied by how far the sensor is from focus, divided by the image distance. Aperture therefore does two jobs at once: it controls how much light gets through, and it controls how much focus error you can tolerate before the picture looks soft.

That trade is what the f-number describes. It is the focal length divided by the aperture diameter, so f/2 is a wide opening and f/16 is a narrow one. Compare Wide open with Stopped down: the second is much sharper away from the focus plane and much darker, which in a real camera is paid for with a longer exposure.

The honest caveats

  • This is two dimensional and paraxial. Real lenses work in three dimensions, and the exact refraction at a curved glass surface is not the linear rule used here.
  • The spherical aberration is modelled, not derived. Rays are given a focal length that shortens with the square of their height on the lens, which reproduces the shape of the defect without coming from a real spherical surface.
  • The lens is treated as infinitely thin, so it has no thickness, no back focal distance and no refractive index.
  • There is no chromatic aberration: every ray here behaves as one wavelength.
  • There is no diffraction. Stopping the aperture down in this model makes the picture sharper without limit, and in a real system diffraction sets a floor on spot size that gets worse as the aperture closes. That limit is a wave effect and needs a different simulation.

Things to try

  • Turn on Keep the sensor in focus and drag the arrow slowly towards the lens. Watch the sensor run away to the right and the image grow. As the object approaches the focal length the sensor distance heads for infinity.
  • Turn that off, focus by hand, then change nothing except the aperture. The image geometry does not move at all. Only the brightness and the size of the blur circle change.
  • Load Spherical aberration and follow a single edge ray. It crosses the axis noticeably before the rays near the centre do, so no sensor position makes the whole cone sharp at once. Then drop the aperture and watch the defect disappear, which is exactly why stopping a cheap lens down improves it.
  • Set the focal length negative to get a diverging lens. There is no object position that produces a real image, which is why a diverging lens is never used alone as a camera lens.
