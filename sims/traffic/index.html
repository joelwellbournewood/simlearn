What am I looking at?

Every car on this ring road follows one rule: get up to your desired speed if the road ahead is clear, and keep a safe gap from the car in front if it is not. There is no accident, no roadworks, no traffic light and nowhere for anyone to turn off. Run it with enough cars and a jam appears anyway, sits still on the map while cars pour into the back of it and dribble out of the front, and drifts slowly backwards around the loop.

The rule, exactly

This is the Intelligent Driver Model. A driver's acceleration is the free part minus the interaction part. The free part pushes towards the desired speed. The interaction part is the ratio between the gap the driver wants and the gap they actually have, squared, so it is almost nothing at a comfortable distance and enormous when the gap collapses. The wanted gap grows with speed, because it is a following time rather than a following distance, and it grows again when you are closing on the car ahead.

Why a jam can start from nothing

Human driving has a delay in it, modelled here as imperfection: a little random wobble on every driver's pedal. On an empty road that wobble dies away. Above a critical density it does the opposite. One driver brakes a shade too hard, the driver behind brakes harder to restore their gap, the next harder still, and a few cars back somebody stops. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why the jam has no cause you can point at from inside your own car.

The two diagrams on the right

The upper one is a space-time diagram, the standard way traffic engineers see a jam. Position around the ring runs left to right, time runs downward, and each streak is one car. Free flow makes a family of parallel diagonal lines. A jam makes a dark band that leans the other way, because the jam moves backwards through the traffic even though every car in it is moving forwards. The slope of that band is close to fifteen kilometres per hour upstream, and it is remarkably insensitive to what the cars are doing.

The lower one is the fundamental diagram. Each dot is a measurement of how many cars pass per hour against how tightly packed they are. Move the car count slider slowly and you draw it yourself. Flow rises with density up to a peak, then falls, and the road delivers fewer cars per hour the more cars you put on it. The peak is capacity, and traffic control is largely the art of keeping a road just below it.

Things worth trying

Set a light density, tap the brakes and watch the disturbance die out. Raise the car count to fifty and tap again, and watch it grow instead. Cut the time headway to make tailgaters and see how much closer to a standstill the same road runs. Then turn adaptive cruise up. A fifth of the cars barely helps and the waves keep coming. It takes most of the road before the stop-and-go flattens out, and with every car under control the loop settles to one steady speed and carries something like a third more cars per hour than the human traffic managed at the same density. Smoothing traffic is not something a small minority can do alone.
