What am I looking at?

One lane of road, bent into a circle. There is no junction, no traffic light, and nowhere to turn off, so nothing can hold a car up except the car in front of it. Every driver follows the same rule all the way round: speed up when the road ahead is clear, and ease off when the gap in front closes to less than feels comfortable at that speed. Put enough cars on the loop and a jam appears anyway, out of nothing.

Watch what the jam actually does. It sits almost still on the tarmac while cars pour into the back of it and dribble out of the front, and it creeps slowly backwards around the loop, against the direction of travel, at a few metres per second. No car is stuck inside it for long. What travels is the congestion itself, a wave moving through the traffic, and the cars are only the medium it moves in.

Each driver decides how hard to accelerate or brake from three things, moment by moment.

  • How far below its preferred speed it is. On an empty road nothing else applies, so the car simply builds up to a cruise.
  • The gap ahead, against the gap it wants. The gap a driver wants grows with speed, because what people really keep is a couple of seconds of road rather than a fixed number of metres. That is the following gap slider.
  • How fast it is closing on the car in front. Gaining on the car ahead produces far harder braking than sitting behind it at a matched speed.

The instability lives in that third quantity. A driver always brakes a little harder than the car in front did, because by the time anyone reacts the gap has already shrunk, and then the driver behind brakes harder still. Every car amplifies the disturbance it inherits, so twenty or thirty cars back a light tap on the pedal has grown into a complete stop. Below a certain density the amplification dies away and the road stays smooth. Above it, the smallest wobble grows on its own, with no crash, no roadworks, and nothing at all to point at. Traffic engineers call the result a phantom jam.

This is the everyday version of the effect: a motorway that flows at nine o'clock and is solid at quarter past, with the same road, the same weather, and much the same drivers. Sitting in one, most people look ahead for the cause and find nothing, which is the correct answer. The cause was a density, reached a few minutes earlier, and a small disturbance that the road was no longer able to absorb.

There are three things to play with. Cars sets the density, and it is the control that decides whether the road is stable at all. Following gap sets how much slack each driver keeps, which changes both how many cars the road can carry and how quickly a disturbance grows. Self-driving cars hands a share of the loop over to drivers that hold a steady gap without over-braking.

Things worth trying

  • Tap the brakes on an open road, then do it again with the cars slider at sixty. One disturbance fades, the other grows.
  • Shorten the following gap. Tailgating fits more cars onto the road, and it also carries every wave further.
  • Raise the self-driving share slowly. A fifth of the cars barely helps and the waves keep coming. It takes most of the road before the stop-and-go flattens out, and with every car under control the loop settles to one steady speed. Smoothing traffic takes a large minority, and a handful of considerate drivers cannot manage it alone.

Start here

The road opens steady. Every driver has a comfortable gap and nothing is going wrong. Press B and one driver, chosen at random, brakes for no reason and then carries on as before. The driver behind brakes a little harder to restore the gap, the next harder still, and a few cars back somebody stops dead. The road never recovers. That single dab of the pedal has become a wave that outlives the car that caused it.

The rule, exactly

This is the Intelligent Driver Model. A driver's acceleration is a free term minus an interaction term. The free term pushes towards the desired speed. The interaction term is the ratio between the gap the driver wants and the gap they actually have, squared, so it is almost nothing at a comfortable distance and enormous once the gap collapses. The wanted gap grows with speed, since it is a following time rather than a following distance, and it grows again whenever the driver is closing on the car ahead. Every human driver also carries a small random wobble on the pedal, which is the seed a jam grows from.

Why the density matters so much

Below about fifty cars on this kilometre the wobble dies away, the road is stable, and a brake tap is absorbed. Above it the same wobble grows instead, and nobody has to touch anything for a jam to appear. That threshold is the whole subject. A road that is fine at nine in the morning and solid at nine fifteen has not changed, and neither have the drivers; only the density has.

The diagram on the right

That is a space-time diagram, the standard way traffic engineers see a jam. Position around the ring runs left to right, time runs downward, and each streak is one car. Free flow makes a family of parallel diagonal lines. A jam makes a dark band leaning the other way, because the congestion moves backwards through the traffic while every car in it is moving forwards. The slope of that band works out at roughly fifteen kilometres per hour upstream, and it barely depends on what the cars are doing.

The car-following model

Each vehicle is an instance of the Intelligent Driver Model (Treiber, Hennecke, & Helbing, 2000). For car α with speed v, gap s to the vehicle ahead, and approach rate Δv:

dv/dt = a [ 1 − (v/v₀)δ − (s*(v,Δv)/s)² ]
s*(v,Δv) = s₀ + max(0, vT + vΔv / (2√(ab)) )

The first bracket is free acceleration toward the desired speed v₀, and the second is the interaction term. s* is the desired minimum gap: a standstill distance s₀, plus the distance covered in the safe time headway T, plus a term that grows with the approach rate and stands for anticipatory braking. The parameters a and b are comfortable acceleration and comfortable deceleration. For reasonable parameters the model is collision-free by construction, and it is continuous, deterministic, and complete, so everything on screen is this one equation applied to every car and integrated in small steps.

The other standard family is discrete. Nagel and Schreckenberg (1992) modelled a road as a cellular automaton in which each car advances, accelerates, brakes to avoid the car ahead, and then, with probability p, slows down for no reason. That single stochastic step is enough to produce jams, which established that stop-and-go traffic requires no difference between drivers, no bottleneck, and no rational cause at all.

String instability, and the critical density

The formal name for what you are watching is string instability, sometimes called convective instability of a platoon. Linearise the car-following equation about a uniform flow in which every car has the same speed and the same gap, then ask how a small perturbation of car n is transmitted to car n+1. Whether its amplitude grows or shrinks along the chain depends on the density and on how sensitively drivers respond to gap and to speed difference. Above the critical density the uniform solution still solves the equations, but the system will not stay in it, and the flow settles instead into a limit cycle of alternating jammed and free clusters. The transition belongs to the whole platoon rather than to any individual car, which is why hunting for the driver who started the jam is the wrong question.

Sugiyama et al. (2008) demonstrated this experimentally by asking twenty-two human drivers to circulate on a 230 m single-lane ring at a uniform speed. The uniform state survived for a short time and then collapsed into a backward-propagating stop-and-go wave, with no obstacle present and no instruction to brake. The experiment is the physical version of exactly this simulation.

The space-time diagram

The plot beside the road is the standard representation in traffic science: position along the road on one axis, time on the other, and each car's world line drawn as a trace. Jams appear as dark stripes with a negative slope, and that slope is the propagation speed of the jam, which in motorway field data comes out consistently around 15 to 20 km/h upstream and is remarkably insensitive to the details of the traffic. Plotting flow against density instead gives the fundamental diagram, which rises to a maximum at the critical density and then falls, so a road carries the most vehicles per hour just before it becomes unstable. Motorways are at their most fragile when they are working hardest. There is also a measurable capacity drop, in which flow after a jam has formed is lower than the flow just before it, and recovering that lost capacity is the reason variable speed limits and ramp metering exist. When a gantry drops the limit to 50 mph on an empty-looking road, it is holding the density below the threshold you can move past with the cars slider.

Autonomous vehicles

Because the instability belongs to the collective response, a minority of vehicles running a different control law can stabilise the whole platoon. Stern et al. (2018) repeated the ring experiment with twenty-one human drivers and one autonomous vehicle running a controller designed to damp waves, and the stop-and-go oscillations were substantially suppressed, with measured reductions in fuel consumption for every vehicle on the ring. The self-driving cars slider is that result. The mechanism is that a controller which refuses to over-brake, and which holds its own speed steady instead of tracking the car in front exactly, breaks the amplification chain. Real deployments complicate the picture, because many production adaptive cruise control systems are tuned for comfort at short headways and have been measured to be string-unstable themselves, so the cars already on the road are not yet the cars in this slider.

Limits of the model

The default road is a single lane with no junctions, so there is no bottleneck of the kind that fixes where real jams form. Switching the second lane on adds overtaking under the MOBIL rule, in which a driver pulls out when it helps them more than it costs the driver they move in front of, and never when it would force that driver to brake hard. Both lanes are treated as the same length, which a real pair of concentric lanes is not, and a drawn ring changes the length of the loop and the speed a car can hold through a bend, and nothing else about the driving. Every vehicle is identical apart from the ones you convert, and no driver makes a mistake beyond the wobble the model imposes. There is no explicit reaction-time delay either, so the lag that drives real instability enters only through the shape of the equation. A ring road is a laboratory device for holding a closed platoon at a fixed density, and a real network behaves differently in ways that matter for planning. The numbers used here sit in the right physical range without being calibrated to any particular road.
