What am I looking at?

Every car on this ring road follows one rule: get up to your desired speed if the road ahead is clear, and keep a safe gap from the car in front if it is not. There is no accident, no roadworks, no traffic light and nowhere to turn off. Run it with enough cars and a jam appears anyway. It sits almost still on the map while cars pour into the back of it and dribble out of the front, and it drifts slowly backwards around the loop.

Start here

The road opens steady. Every driver has a comfortable gap and nothing is going wrong. Press B and one driver, chosen at random, brakes for no reason at all and then carries on. The driver behind brakes a little harder to restore the gap, the next harder still, and a few cars back somebody stops. The road never recovers: that one dab of the brake pedal becomes a wave that outlives the car that caused it.

The rule, exactly

This is the Intelligent Driver Model. A driver's acceleration is the free part minus the interaction part. The free part pushes towards the desired speed. The interaction part is the ratio between the gap the driver wants and the gap they actually have, squared, so it is almost nothing at a comfortable distance and enormous when the gap collapses. The wanted gap grows with speed, because it is a following time rather than a following distance, and it grows again when you are closing on the car ahead. Every human driver also carries a small random wobble on the pedal, which is the seed a jam grows from.

Why the density matters so much

Below about fifty cars on this kilometre the wobble dies away: the road is stable and a brake tap is absorbed. Above it the same wobble grows instead, and you do not have to touch anything for a jam to appear. That threshold is the whole subject. A road that is fine at nine in the morning and solid at nine fifteen has not changed, and neither have the drivers.

The diagram on the right

That is a space-time diagram, the standard way traffic engineers see a jam. Position around the ring runs left to right, time runs downward, and each streak is one car. Free flow makes a family of parallel diagonal lines. A jam makes a dark band leaning the other way, because the jam moves backwards through the traffic even though every car in it is moving forwards. The slope of that band is close to fifteen kilometres per hour upstream, and it barely depends on what the cars are doing.

Things worth trying

  • Tap the brakes on the open road, then again with the cars slider at sixty. Watch one disturbance die and the other grow.
  • Shorten the following gap. Tailgating fits more cars on the road and makes every wave travel further.
  • Push the self-driving share up slowly. A fifth of the cars barely helps and the waves keep coming. It takes most of the road before the stop-and-go flattens out, and with every car under control the loop settles to one steady speed. Smoothing traffic is not something a small minority can do alone.
