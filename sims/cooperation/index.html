Cooperators & Competitors

Every cell on this lattice is a player with one habit. A cooperator pays a cost so that its neighbours do better. A competitor pays nothing and takes what is going. Each generation every player collects what its habit earned against the neighbours it has, and then the lattice updates: players look at what the people around them earned, and the habit that did best spreads into the cells around it.

Held on its own, the arithmetic is bleak. Against any single neighbour, refusing to pay the cost always earns more than paying it, so competing wins every pairwise contest and cooperation should vanish. It has not vanished. Cells in a tissue divide at a rate that suits the tissue, bacteria release enzymes that feed the whole colony, and people give blood. The question this model asks is narrow and answerable: what has to be true of the neighbourhood for the expensive habit to survive?

The first answer is on screen the moment you press play. Cooperators survive by sticking together. A cooperator in a block of cooperators is surrounded by others paying in, so it does well, while a competitor on the edge of that block feeds off it and does better still. The block is eaten at the edge and grows from the middle, and whether it grows or shrinks depends on how steep the temptation is. Drop one competitor into a full field of cooperators with One cheat in Eden and the fight between those two effects draws itself as a shifting fractal, which is the picture Nowak and May published in 1992.

Things to try

  • Run One cheat in Eden and raise Temptation to cheat slowly. Somewhere just below 2 the growing cooperator blocks stop growing, and everything collapses within a few dozen generations.
  • Switch to Nobody has neighbours. Same game, same temptation, same copying rule, but every player now meets eight random strangers drawn afresh each generation instead of the cells beside it. Cooperation falls from about a third of the field to under a tenth, and what survives is luck rather than structure.
  • On Sharing a shovel, the snowdrift game, watch the shape of what survives. Cooperators hold about half the field, in filaments rather than blocks, because here it pays to be next to somebody unlike you. Then push the cost of shovelling to 0.8. A well-mixed crowd would settle at one fifth cooperators, and this lattice settles at about one eighth, so the neighbourhood has made cooperation worse, which is the Hauert and Doebeli result.
  • Start Moving house and turn Urge to move down to never. Cooperation collapses to well under a percent within a few hundred generations. Put it back up to always and it recovers to about half the field, without anybody ever inspecting anybody's strategy.
  • On Loners keep them honest, follow one spiral arm. Free riders chase cooperators, loners starve the free riders, and cooperators come back into the space the loners left.
  • Paint a solid square of cooperators about ten cells across into a field of competitors, then a thin line of the same number. The square survives and the line is eaten.

The three games

The dilemma. Mutual cooperation pays 1 each, a competitor meeting a cooperator collects the temptation b, the exploited cooperator gets nothing, and two competitors get nothing. This is the weak prisoner's dilemma used by Nowak and May, and its one parameter is the whole story on a lattice.

Snowdrift. Two drivers are stuck behind the same drift. If both dig, each pays half the effort and both drive home. If one digs, the digger pays the whole cost and both still drive home, so the passenger does best of all. If neither digs, both sit there. Because being the only cooperator still beats mutual refusal, cooperation persists without any help from structure, and Hauert and Doebeli showed in 2004 that a lattice can actively make it worse. That result matters, because it is a case where clustering hurts, and it is measurable in the laboratory: Gore and colleagues found in 2009 that budding yeast splitting sucrose play a snowdrift game rather than a prisoner's dilemma.

The public pot. Everyone who joins in contributes one unit, the pot is multiplied and shared equally among the joiners, and a loner takes a small fixed amount instead of playing. When the multiplier is smaller than the group, contributing is irrational, so free riders take over, and once nearly everyone is a free rider the pot is worth less than opting out, so loners take over, and a field of loners has nothing to exploit, so cooperators come back. Nobody wins for long. Hauert, De Monte, Hofbauer, and Sigmund named this the Red Queen mechanism in 2002.

How the update rule changes the answer

Copy the best is deterministic, and every cell adopts the habit of whichever cell in its neighbourhood earned most, including itself. It is the rule that produces the crisp fractal patterns, and it is fragile in an instructive way, because a single very lucky cell can convert a whole neighbourhood. Copy by chance is the Fermi rule of Szabo and Toke, in which a cell picks one neighbour and switches with a probability that grows with the payoff gap. It is noisy, it never freezes, and it is closer to what an economist would call imitation under uncertainty. Most published phase diagrams for these games use it.

The theory behind the pictures has a clean summary. Ohtsuki, Hauert, Lieberman, and Nowak showed in 2006 that on a graph where every player has k neighbours, cooperation is favoured when the benefit-to-cost ratio of the helping act exceeds k. Fewer neighbours, more cooperation. It is the sharpest statement of why the neighbourhood, and not the goodwill of the players, decides this.

Moving without judging

The urge to move implements success-driven migration, from Helbing and Yu in 2009. A player looks at the empty ground within reach, works out what each vacant spot would have paid it, and moves to the best one if it beats staying. No player ever asks what strategy anybody else is playing, and no player intends to build a cooperative cluster. Cooperators nevertheless end up beside cooperators, because that is where the payoff is, and a population that would collapse without movement can survive with it. The same paper showed the effect is strongest with a moderate amount of noise, which is why the scenario ships with a few stubborn mistakes turned on.

Limits of the model

Every player here has exactly one fixed habit, no memory of who did what, and no way of recognising an individual, which rules out the whole family of mechanisms built on reputation and on returning a favour. Real cooperation leans on those heavily. Payoffs are collected as a plain sum over neighbours and treated as fitness directly, with no birth, no death, and no age, so a cell "reproducing" is only a neighbour changing habit. The lattice is a torus of identical sites, which is the one geometry that no real population has, and the results are known to shift on other graphs and to shift again if the update is synchronous rather than one cell at a time. Migration here samples up to twenty vacant sites within the vision radius rather than examining every one, which keeps a large lattice responsive and makes moves slightly less optimal than in the published model. Nothing on screen is calibrated to any particular organism or market, and the numbers are payoff units, not currency. What the model does carry over is the structural claim, which is that the survival of an expensive habit is decided by who you are next to and how easily you can move.
