What am I looking at?

In 1963 Edward Lorenz reduced a model of atmospheric convection to three numbers: how fast the fluid is rolling, and two measures of how the heat is distributed across it. Three equations, with no randomness and no external forcing. He expected the solution to settle down or to repeat. It did neither.

The moving point is the state of that system drawn in three dimensions. It never crosses its own path and it never escapes the region. It winds around one lobe for a while, flips to the other, winds there, flips back, indefinitely, in an order that has no pattern.

Why the picture mattered

Before Lorenz, an aperiodic wiggle in a model was put down to noise or to too few decimal places. He produced one in a system with neither excuse. The set the path is drawn onto is a strange attractor: everything nearby is pulled towards it, and yet two points on it that start a millionth apart end up on opposite lobes. It attracts and it stays unpredictable.

This is also the origin of the butterfly. The shape is a coincidence; the substance is Lorenz's question about whether a flap in Brazil could set off a tornado in Texas. Press Swarm of fifty and watch fifty starts a thousandth apart become fifty unrelated futures.

The parameter that decides the behaviour

ρ is the Rayleigh number: a measure of how hard the fluid is being heated.

  • Below 1: nothing moves. Heat conducts through. Every start collapses to the origin.
  • Between 1 and about 24.74: steady convection. The path spirals in and stops at one of two fixed points, one for each direction of roll. Try ρ = 14.
  • Above 24.74: both fixed points lose stability and there is nowhere left to settle. Chaos. Lorenz used 28.
  • Around 99.5 to 100.8: a periodic window. The chaos gives way to a clean repeating loop, with chaos again on either side. Try ρ = 100.5.

The honest caveats

  • Integration is fourth-order Runge-Kutta. It has to be: with a first-order method the path visibly drifts off the attractor and physics cannot be told from arithmetic.
  • This is not weather. It is three variables where the atmosphere has billions. It demonstrates that unpredictability can be built into a system; it does not model anything you would forecast with.
  • The separation readout is the largest distance between any two of the released paths in state space. On a chaotic setting it grows roughly exponentially until it saturates at the size of the attractor.

Things to try

  • Set ρ to 14 and restart. Watch the spiral tighten and stop. Then move it to 25 and restart. The equations have not changed shape, and the behaviour has.
  • Release the swarm, then read the lobe swaps counter. The sequence of left and right is as good a coin flip as anything in physics, and it comes out of arithmetic with no randomness in it.
  • Drag the view until you are looking down the attractor's axis. The two lobes are not flat discs; they are sheets that never quite touch.
