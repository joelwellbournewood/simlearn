What am I looking at?

Every body on screen pulls on every other one, with the law Newton published in 1687: the force grows with both masses and falls away with the square of the distance between them. That single line of arithmetic is the whole program, applied to every pair of bodies, thousands of times a second.

Nothing in the code knows the word orbit, or galaxy, or Lagrange point. No rule anywhere makes a planet go round in an ellipse. What each body does is only this, at every instant:

  • Add up the pulls. Every other body contributes an acceleration toward itself, proportional to its own mass and to one over the square of its distance.
  • Change velocity by that acceleration, then change position by that velocity. There is nothing else. Mass, position, velocity.

Everything else on screen is a consequence. An ellipse is what you get when the pull is exactly balanced by the tendency to fly off in a straight line, and the ellipse's shape follows from the speed you happened to give the body. A body that is going a little too fast leaves and never comes back. Three bodies of similar mass cannot settle into anything at all, and usually resolve the problem by throwing one member out. Stable points appear in a rotating two-body system where the pulls cancel. Two discs passing each other tear long tails out of one another, because the near side of a disc is pulled harder than the far side.

This is also the oldest working example of a model earning its keep. Newton's law was used to predict the existence and the position of Neptune before anyone had seen it, in 1846, and the same arithmetic now flies every spacecraft that leaves the ground. Mission designers even make deliberate use of the messy three-body behaviour on display here: the near-cancelling pulls around the Lagrange points open up very slow, very cheap routes between orbits, and in 1991 the Japanese probe Hiten reached the Moon on one of them with almost no fuel left.

Kepler's laws come out on their own

Click any body. The dashed curve is the path it would follow if only its main attractor existed: an ellipse if it is bound to that body, a hyperbola if it is passing through. The card gives you its eccentricity, its semi-major axis, and its period. Watch the comet in Star and planets. It crawls at the far end of its ellipse and whips around the near end, sweeping out equal areas in equal times, which is Kepler's second law. Compare any two planets and you will find the period grows as the semi-major axis to the power of one and a half, which is his third. Kepler spent years extracting those rules from Tycho Brahe's observations. Here they arrive free with the inverse square law.

Why two bodies are easy and three are not

Two bodies have an exact solution. Add a third and no such formula exists. Henri Poincare showed in the 1890s that the general three-body problem is chaotic, so two starts a hair apart end up in completely different places. Pythagorean three is the classic demonstration, posed by Burrau in 1913 and only followed through to the end numerically by Szebehely and Peters in 1967. Masses of 3, 4, and 5 start at rest on the corners of a 3-4-5 triangle, and each one carries its number on screen so you can follow which is which. The view tracks the survivors, so when a body is thrown clear the camera stays with the pair it left behind. After a run of close passes one body is flung out and the other two settle into a binary. That is the usual ending. A three-body system resolves its own chaos by throwing a member away.

The figure-eight

Figure-eight is one of the few periodic solutions known for three equal masses. Cristopher Moore found it numerically in 1993, and Chenciner and Montgomery proved in 2000 that it genuinely exists. All three bodies chase each other around the same lemniscate, each a third of a period behind the one in front. It survives small nudges, which is why it holds together here. No real system has ever been seen doing it. Add a few small bodies with the slider, or sling one through the middle, and see how long the choreography lasts.

Lagrange points

Put a star and a planet on a circular orbit and there are exactly five places where a third, much lighter body can sit and keep station with them, going round once per planet year and so never moving relative to the pair. They are the points where the star's pull, the planet's pull, and the centrifugal effect of going round together all cancel out. Euler found the three on the line through the two bodies in the 1760s. Lagrange found the other two in 1772.

Where they are. Switch the view to co-rotating (this arrangement starts that way) and the star, the planet, and all five points stand still, which is the only frame in which they make sense. The dashed circle is the planet's orbit.

  • L1 sits between the star and the planet, about 15% of the way back toward the star. It is where the planet's pull cancels part of the star's.
  • L2 sits the same sort of distance directly beyond the planet, away from the star.
  • L3 sits on the far side of the star, almost exactly as far from it as the planet is (0.994 of the separation, from the numbers this arrangement runs on), so it lands within a hair of the dashed orbit circle, permanently hidden from the planet behind the star.
  • L4 and L5 sit on the planet's own orbit, 60 degrees ahead of it and 60 degrees behind. Each one makes an equilateral triangle with the star and the planet, which is why the faint triangle is drawn to them. They are the two furthest from the pair, and the only two that lie off the line.

Stable or not. L4 and L5 (marked teal) are genuinely stable when the planet is lighter than about a twenty-fifth of the star: nudge a body off them and the Coriolis effect curls it back into a slow loop around the point. That is why Jupiter's Trojan asteroids have held those two spots for billions of years, and why the six small bodies drawn there in this arrangement librate rather than escape. L1, L2, and L3 (marked gold) are saddle points, balanced on a ridge, so anything left there slides away within an orbit or two. The James Webb telescope sits at Sun-Earth L2 and fires thrusters every few weeks to stay there, and the fuel it carries for those burns is what sets the end of its mission.

Horseshoe orbits

The Horseshoe orbits arrangement shows the other family of co-orbital motion, and it needs a lighter planet than the one in the Lagrange scene. Each companion starts on a circle about two percent wider or two percent narrower than the planet's, which is enough to make it drift slowly past in the rotating view, and little enough that the planet can turn it back well before it arrives.

Follow the narrow one. It goes round slightly faster, so it gains on the planet from behind. The planet, ahead of it, pulls it forward, which adds energy and lifts it into a wider and therefore slower orbit, and from there it falls behind again. Coming up on the planet from the other side, the pull is backwards, energy is taken away, and it drops back to the narrow orbit. Neither companion ever gets close, and in the rotating view each traces a long horseshoe whose ends stop well short of the planet. One lap of the shape takes a few hundred orbits, so push the time speed up and let the trail draw it.

Saturn's moons Janus and Epimetheus are on exactly this dance, about fifty kilometres apart in orbital radius, and they trade orbits every four years without ever coming within fifteen thousand kilometres of one another. Earth has a horseshoe companion too, the asteroid 2010 SO16, which takes hundreds of years to make one pass of the shape. The narrowness of the band matters: too close to the planet's own orbit and the companion settles into a tadpole around L4 or L5 instead, too far and the planet cannot turn it back at all, so it crosses the orbit and is scattered.

The star here is 100 times the mass of the planet, which is a heavier planet than any in the solar system. That is deliberate, because it puts L1 and L2 far enough from the planet to see. The point positions are computed from the current separation of the two heaviest bodies, so if you sling a new heavy body in, the crosses move to where they now belong.

The slingshot

Send a light body past a heavy one that is itself moving and it can leave far faster than it arrived. Nothing is created. The speed is borrowed from the heavy body's orbital motion, which slows by an amount far too small to measure. Voyager 2 reached Neptune on a chain of these assists, and every mission to the outer planets since has been designed around the same trick, which is why launch windows for them come round only every few years. Drag on empty space to line one up, and the dotted line previews where your body will go.

Tidal tails

In 1972 Alar and Juri Toomre showed, using a few hundred test particles on a computer weaker than the one in your pocket, that the bridges and streamers around peculiar galaxies are tidal. A passing galaxy pulls harder on the near side of a disc than on the far side, stretching it both ways at once, and the disc's own rotation winds the debris into long arcs. Galaxy and intruder and Galaxy collision follow that recipe. Encounters where the disc spins the same way it orbits throw the longest tails. Stars in a real galaxy almost never hit each other, so leave collisions off in these two. The discs pass straight through, and gravity alone does all the damage.

Mergers and the growth of big things

Merge on contact, under Physics, combines two bodies that touch into one, conserving mass and momentum. Energy is lost, because a real collision turns motion into heat. It starts switched off in every arrangement except Cold collapse, where growth by accretion is the whole point, and off is the more interesting setting for the rest: a body that would have been swallowed instead whips round and comes back on a new orbit. Turn it on and you get the asymmetry that built the planets, which is that the heaviest body pulls in the most material and so grows fastest, until a cloud of near-equals has become a few large winners with leftovers around them.

Building your own arrangement

Under the bonnet

  • Each body is treated as a softened sphere rather than a point, so the force stops growing once two bodies are closer than their own size. That is what prevents infinities during a close pass. The Pythagorean problem depends on very close approaches, so its exact timing here will differ from the published solution while the story stays the same.
  • Motion is integrated with velocity Verlet, and each frame is cut into as many substeps as the fiercest acceleration on screen demands. Total energy holds to well under a percent through ordinary orbits, so what you see separating is physics rather than arithmetic.
  • Past about 400 bodies the forces are computed with a Barnes-Hut tree, which lumps distant groups together and cuts the cost from n squared to n log n, at a force error near one percent. This is the same idea used in professional galaxy codes.
  • Everything is two dimensional. Discs are flat anyway, and real galaxies also carry dark matter and gas, so the rotation here falls off in the Keplerian way instead of staying flat as it does in the sky.

The mathematics being integrated

For N bodies with masses mi at positions ri, the whole model is

r̈i = −G ∑j≠i mj (ri − rj) / (|ri − rj|² + ε²)3/2

The ε is a softening length, standing in for the physical size of a body. Without it the force diverges during a close pass and the integrator loses accuracy or blows up. Softening is standard practice in astrophysical simulation, and it changes the dynamics at short range, which is worth remembering whenever a result depends on a very close approach.

Two bodies reduce exactly to the Kepler problem, which has a closed-form solution: bound orbits are conic sections, and Kepler's three laws follow from conservation of angular momentum and energy. For N = 3 there is no general closed-form solution. Bruns and Poincare showed in the 1880s and 1890s that no further algebraic integrals of the required kind exist, and Poincare's work on the restricted three-body problem, which was where he found homoclinic tangles, is the origin of dynamical chaos as a subject. Two facts have lasted. The three-body problem is chaotic for generic initial conditions, and its typical outcome is disintegration, with one body ejected and the remaining two forming a binary. Special periodic solutions exist and occupy a set of measure zero, which is why the figure-eight orbit, found numerically by Moore (1993) and proved to exist by Chenciner and Montgomery (2000), has never been observed in the sky.

Numerical method and cost

Motion is integrated with velocity Verlet, a symplectic scheme that conserves phase-space volume and keeps energy error bounded and oscillatory instead of letting it grow steadily, which is why total energy holds to well under a percent through ordinary orbits here. Each frame is subdivided into as many substeps as the largest acceleration on screen requires. The live energy drift readout exists so that a separation you see can be attributed to the physics, since in a chaotic configuration physics and arithmetic are indistinguishable without it.

Direct summation costs O(N²) force evaluations per step. Past a few hundred bodies this simulation switches to a Barnes-Hut tree (Barnes & Hut, 1986), which groups distant bodies into cells and replaces each with its centre of mass whenever the cell subtends a small enough angle, reducing the cost to O(N log N) for a force error near a percent. Professional galaxy and cosmology codes use the same idea, or the fast multipole method, for the same reason.

Where the specific set pieces come from

The five equilibrium points of the circular restricted three-body problem were found by Euler, for the three collinear points, and by Lagrange in 1772 for the triangular pair. L4 and L5 are linearly stable when the mass ratio of the secondary to the total is below the Routh value of about 0.0385, roughly one twenty-fifth, a condition established by Gascheau in 1843. The Coriolis term in the rotating frame is what curls a displaced body back into a libration orbit instead of letting it fall away. Jupiter's Trojan asteroids and the Earth's tadpole companions occupy those points. L1, L2, and L3 are saddle points, so spacecraft placed there, including the James Webb Space Telescope at Sun-Earth L2, must perform regular station-keeping burns. The tidal bridges and tails in the galaxy arrangements follow Toomre and Toomre (1972), who showed with a few hundred test particles that the streamers of peculiar galaxies are gravitational tides from a close passage, with the longest tails produced when the disc's spin is prograde with respect to the encounter.

Limits of the model

Everything here is two-dimensional and purely Newtonian, with no relativistic precession and no radiation, so a tight binary will never spiral in as a real one does. There is also no gas and no dark matter halo, which is why galaxy rotation in these scenes falls off in the Keplerian way instead of staying flat as observed rotation curves do. Collisions, when you switch them on, are treated as perfectly inelastic mergers conserving mass and momentum, a crude stand-in for accretion that is simply wrong for stars, which is one reason they are off by default. The units are arbitrary, so no number on screen is in kilograms or seconds. What the model is good for is structural: one inverse-square law, applied to everything at once, is enough to generate the whole catalogue of orbital behaviour.
