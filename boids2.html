Under the hood

Every boid looks only at neighbours inside its perception radius and field of view, then follows three rules: separation (steer away from crowding), alignment (match neighbours' heading) and cohesion (drift toward their centre). Nothing is coordinating the flock; the patterns are emergent.

Starlings don't watch everyone within a radius; each bird tracks its six or seven nearest neighbours however far away they are. That topological rule is what keeps a murmuration one body and lets a turn ripple across it, so the Murmuration preset uses it.

Two more things from the field: an escape wave travels because birds copy neighbours that are turning hard (startle), and most flock-wide turns begin with a single bird on the edge deciding to turn (initiative).

Order in the corner is the average heading agreement: 1 means the whole flock flies the same way, 0 means no shared direction at all.

What am I looking at?

A flock with no leader. Every arrow is a boid — short for "bird-oid object" — a tiny simulated animal that can only see its nearest neighbours and follows three rules. Nobody tells the flock where to go, yet it moves as one shape, splits, rejoins and dodges. Behaviour that appears at the level of the group without being programmed into any individual is called emergence, and this is one of the cleanest examples of it.

The three rules

  • Separation — steer away from neighbours that are too close, so nobody collides.
  • Alignment — steer toward the average heading of your neighbours.
  • Cohesion — steer toward the average position of your neighbours, so you don't get left behind.

Each boid mixes these three pulls every frame, using only what it can see inside its perception radius and field of view. Change the balance with the sliders and the flock's personality changes completely: lots of alignment gives you streams and sheets, lots of cohesion gives you tight balls, lots of separation gives you a loose gas.

Where it comes from

Craig Reynolds published the boids model in 1987 to animate flocks for computer graphics. It was used for the bat swarms and penguin crowds in Batman Returns (1992), and it became a foundation for studying collective behaviour in birds, fish, insects and even crowds of people. Later fieldwork on real starlings found something the original model didn't have: each bird tracks its six or seven nearest neighbours, however far away they are, rather than everyone within a fixed distance. They also found that flock-wide turns usually begin with one bird on the edge, and spread through the flock faster than any bird flies. The Murmuration preset uses those findings, plus a roost to circle and a falcon to flee from.

Things to try

  • Hold the pointer on the flock with Attract, then switch to Repel and scatter it.
  • Drop a predator and watch fear ripple through the group.
  • Draw an obstacle and see the flock split around it and re-form.
  • Turn on "Show one boid's view" under the hood to see exactly what a single boid knows.
  • Watch the order number: it measures how much of the flock agrees on a direction.
