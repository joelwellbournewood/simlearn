What am I looking at?

A square of tissue with two chemicals in it. One of them, the activator, makes more of itself and eats the other. The second, the substrate, is pumped in from outside and spreads about twice as fast. That is the entire mechanism, and it is enough to produce spots, stripes, branching coral, dividing blobs and travelling waves without anyone drawing them.

Turing's idea

In 1952 Alan Turing pointed out something counterintuitive: diffusion, which normally smooths differences out, can create them. If a self-amplifying chemical is held in check by a second one that spreads faster, a uniform mixture becomes unstable. Any tiny fluctuation grows locally while suppressing its neighbourhood, and the system settles into a pattern with a characteristic spacing. Turing called the chemicals morphogens and suggested they were how embryos decide where to put things.

This particular pair is the Gray–Scott model, an autocatalytic reaction in a stirred tank rewritten as a field. u is the substrate, v the activator, and the reaction is u + 2v → 3v.

∂u/∂t = Du∇²u − uv² + F(1 − u) ∂v/∂t = Dv∇²v + uv² − (F + k)v

F is the feed rate, the rate at which substrate is topped up towards 1. k is the extra kill rate on the activator. Those two numbers are the only things the presets change.

Why the map has that shape

The two curves on the parameter map are not decoration; they are solved from the equations with the diffusion switched off.

  • The upper arc is where the non-trivial steady states collide and vanish. Above it, the only uniform state is the dead one, u = 1 and v = 0. Patterns still live up there, but only as isolated structures that must keep dividing to survive, which is why the mitosis preset sits just above the line.
  • The lower curve is a Hopf bifurcation. To its left the steady state loses stability by oscillating rather than by splitting in space, so instead of a static pattern you get travelling waves and chemical turbulence. The waves and chaos presets sit right on it.
  • The olive patch is the only region where a classical Turing instability actually applies: a uniform steady state that is stable on its own but goes unstable once diffusion is switched on. Unlike the two curves, this one does depend on how much faster the substrate spreads. At the default ratio of 2 it is a thin sliver on the right; drag the diffusion ratio up and watch it open out across the map.

Every preset in the list sits within a few thousandths of one of the two curves. The whole zoo lives in a sliver, which is why the map is worth navigating by clicking rather than by typing numbers.

The growing field

Real embryos are not fixed squares. A somite-stage vertebrate trunk, a fish flank, a limb bud: all of them are patterning while getting bigger. When you switch on growth here, the tissue stretches lengthwise while the chemistry keeps its own preferred spacing. The result is that stripes do not stretch with the animal. New ones insert between the old ones, which is exactly what was observed in the marine angelfish Pomacanthus by Kondo and Asai in 1995: as the fish grows, its stripes keep constant width and new stripes appear in between, rather than the existing pattern simply scaling up.

The field is drawn in material coordinates, so what you see is the tissue itself elongating. Watch the stripe count in the readout climb while the visible spacing stays put.

Directional diffusion

Diffusion in tissue is often faster along one axis than another, because cells are elongated, or aligned, or the extracellular matrix is fibrous. Turning on directional diffusion replaces the single diffusion constant with a tensor. Structures then line up: the pattern's wavelength is longer along the fast axis, so stripes tend to run across it. The strength is capped at 3× because beyond that the explicit solver used here stops being stable.

Are these actually Turing patterns?

Partly, and it is worth being precise about it. Turing's own argument is a linear one: take a uniform steady state that is stable on its own, add diffusion, show that a perturbation now grows. That condition is the olive patch on the map, and at the standard diffusion ratio of 2 not one of the twelve presets falls inside it. Most of them sit where no non-trivial uniform state exists at all, or where the state oscillates. What you are watching in those cases is not a uniform mixture going unstable; it is a self-sustaining localized structure, a pulse of activator that eats outward, splits when it gets too broad, and starves where the substrate has run out.

That gap is why Pearson's 1993 paper on this model is called Complex patterns in a simple system. The ingredients are Turing's and so is the characteristic spacing, but the linear analysis Turing wrote down does not by itself explain a spot that divides. Push the diffusion ratio above about 3 and the olive region swells to cover the right-hand presets, which is the regime where the textbook version does hold.

What this is not

Gray–Scott is a caricature. No animal runs this exact reaction. Whether Turing mechanisms actually set real body patterns was argued over for decades, and the answer turned out to be yes in some places: zebrafish pigment stripes arise from short-range and long-range interactions between pigment cell types, mouse digit number follows a Turing-like Bmp/Sox9/Wnt network, and hair follicle spacing depends on a Wnt/Dkk activator–inhibitor pair. In those systems the "diffusing" species are often cells or signalling ranges rather than freely diffusing molecules, but the mathematics is the same family.

Under the hood

  • Explicit forward Euler on a periodic grid, so the left edge wraps to the right and the top to the bottom.
  • A nine-point diffusion stencil, generalised to a diffusion tensor so that the directional and growth modes stay stable.
  • Growth is handled in material coordinates: the diffusion term along the growing axis is divided by the square of the stretch factor, and both chemicals are diluted at the rate the tissue expands.
  • The whole thing runs on the GPU in a pair of floating-point textures.
