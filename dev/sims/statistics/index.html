What am I looking at?

Six small machines, each built from nothing but raw chance, and each producing something you can predict. A single coin flip is as unpredictable as anything in nature. A million coin flips are so predictable that casinos, insurers, pollsters, and particle physicists stake their livelihoods on them. This page is about the road between those two facts.

Pick a machine on the left. Each one runs a genuinely random process in front of you, physical where that helps, and shows the raw randomness and the structure it settles into side by side.

  • Galton board. Balls rattle down through rows of pegs, bouncing left or right at each one with no memory and no plan. The bins underneath fill into a bell curve, every time, and the curve drawn over the bins was computed before a single ball fell.
  • Sample means. Draws from a population of any shape you like, flat, lopsided, twin peaked, or a bare coin, are averaged in small handfuls, and each average falls into a histogram. The histogram of averages comes out bell shaped whatever the population looked like. This is the central limit theorem, and it is the reason the bell curve is everywhere.
  • Confidence intervals. A truth is fixed, an experiment measures it with a small sample, and the measurement is wrapped in an interval that claims 95 percent confidence. Watch a hundred experiments stack up and count how many intervals actually caught the truth. Then hide the truth and notice that your own interval, the one experiment you actually ran, never tells you whether it was one of the unlucky ones.
  • Law of large numbers. A coin is flipped up to ten thousand times a second. The running fraction of heads settles onto the true bias, while the raw surplus of heads over tails wanders further and further from zero. Both curves are drawn, because the difference between them is exactly the mistake the gambler's fallacy is made of.
  • Random walkers. Hundreds of particles each take one blind step at a time, and the swarm spreads into a cone that grows with the square root of time. This is diffusion, seen from close enough to watch the individual accidents it is made of.
  • Monte Carlo darts. Darts thrown blindly at a square measure the area of the circle inside it, and out of pure noise comes a steadily sharpening estimate of π. Randomness used as an instrument.

One idea runs through all six. Individual random events stay lawless, and no streak, hunch, or system changes that. Aggregates of random events obey laws as reliable as mechanics. Everything from opinion polling and drug trials to noise in electronics and the price of your insurance rests on that single asymmetry.

Things to try

  • On the Galton board, drag the funnel sideways. The bell follows it, because the bell's position remembers the start while its width remembers only the pegs. Then push the peg bias to 0.8 and watch the bell walk right and lean.
  • In sample means, set the population to Coin and draws per sample to 1. The histogram is two bare spikes, about as far from a bell as a distribution can get. Now slide draws per sample up to 30 and watch a bell assemble itself out of coin flips.
  • In confidence intervals, drop the sample size to 3 and switch to the z shortcut. The tally sinks toward 80 percent while still claiming 95, which is the exact mistake Student's t was invented to fix, and the reason it exists on every stats formula sheet.
  • In the law of large numbers, wait for ten thousand flips and look at the two charts together: the fraction pinned to the bias, the surplus drifting tens of heads away from zero. A coin that has just produced seven tails owes you nothing.
  • In random walkers, hold the pointer to one side of the cone and you become the wind. Let go and the cone forgets you at the rate the envelope allows, and no faster.

Why pegs make a bell

Each peg gives a ball one nudge, left or right, independent of every nudge before it. After n rows the ball's bin is simply the number of rightward nudges, so the bin counts follow the binomial distribution B(n, p). De Moivre showed in 1733 that this distribution approaches the normal curve as n grows, and the board makes his theorem a physical object: the curve drawn over the bins is

mean μ = np, spread σ = √(np(1−p))

computed from the slider values alone, before any ball has landed. The readout under the bins compares the balls' measured mean and spread against those predictions, and the agreement typically sets in within a few hundred balls.

The central limit theorem

The Galton board averages coin flips. The second machine averages draws from any distribution with a finite variance, and the theorem says the result is the same: the mean of n independent draws from a population with mean μ and standard deviation σ approaches a normal distribution

x̄ ∼ N(μ, σ/√n)

whatever the population's shape. Two things are worth watching for. The bell appears fast, with n around 30 already convincing even for the lopsided populations, which is where the folklore threshold of 30 in statistics courses comes from. And the bell narrows like 1/√n, so each doubling of sample size buys only a factor of 1.41 in precision. That square root governs the cost of every poll, survey, and clinical trial: to halve the error you must quadruple the sample, which is why precision is expensive and why polls stop near a thousand respondents.

What 95 percent confidence actually promises

An interval built from a sample of n measurements is

x̄ ± tν · s/√n

where s is the sample's own spread and tν is Student's critical value with ν = n−1 degrees of freedom. Neyman's 1937 formulation, the one this machine animates, promises only this: the procedure, run many times, catches the truth in 95 percent of runs. The promise belongs to the factory, never to the single interval in your hand, which either contains the truth or does not. The Hidden setting makes that concrete, since the intervals look identical when you cannot see μ, and real experimenters never can. The t versus z switch shows why the small print matters: the z shortcut treats the sample's spread as if it were the truth, its intervals come out less than half the honest length at n = 3, and the coverage tally sinks to the low eighties while still claiming 95.

Averages settle, totals wander

The law of large numbers says the fraction of heads converges to p. It says nothing about the count of heads minus the count of tails, and that count follows a random walk whose typical size grows as √n, without bound. After a run of tails the coin does not owe heads; the fraction recovers by dilution, not by correction. Roulette systems, hot hands at slot machines, and the feeling that a lottery number is due are all bets against this distinction, and the envelope drawn on both charts is the house's side of the argument.

Diffusion is the same mathematics wearing physics

A walker's position after t steps is a sum of t random nudges, so the swarm's spread is σ√t, the same square root as the sample mean's error and the Galton bell's width. Einstein's 1905 analysis of Brownian motion used exactly this argument to predict how far a pollen grain should wander, Perrin measured it, and the agreement was the evidence that finally made atoms respectable. Add drift and you get transport plus spreading, which is how smoke leaves a chimney, how a drug spreads from an injection site, and why contamination plumes in groundwater grow long before they grow wide.

Randomness as an instrument

The dart board estimates π as 4 times the fraction of uniform darts landing inside the circle. The estimate's error shrinks as 1/√N, slowly, and that slowness is the honest price printed on every Monte Carlo method. The technique earns its keep where honest alternatives are worse: integrals over hundreds of dimensions, neutron transport, financial risk, and rendering the light in animated films are all computed by throwing darts, because in high dimensions a random sample beats any grid you could afford.

How this one is computed

  • Randomness comes from the browser's pseudo-random generator, with normal draws made by the Box-Muller transform. Nothing is staged, so runs differ every time, and a slow start on the coverage tally is chance doing what chance does.
  • Galton balls decide left or right once per row, which makes the bin counts exactly binomial. The bounce you watch is animation over that decision, so the physics look does not disturb the arithmetic the theory curve is checked against.
  • Student's t critical values come from a series approximation in 1/ν accurate to a few parts in a thousand for ν ≥ 2, and the normal quantile from a standard rational approximation.
  • Every overlay curve is computed from the sliders before the data lands, never fitted to the data afterwards, because the whole point is prediction.

Limits of the model

Everything here assumes draws that are independent and identically distributed, and most statistical disasters in the wild are failures of that assumption rather than of arithmetic. Survey respondents cluster, financial returns move together in a crash, and one contaminated batch correlates a thousand measurements, so real errors shrink slower than √n promises, and sometimes barely shrink at all. The central limit theorem also needs a finite variance, and heavy-tailed quantities such as insurance losses from natural catastrophes or market moves keep their skew far longer than the folklore n of 30 suggests. And a confidence interval guards only against sampling luck, never against a biased instrument or a leading question, which no sample size can fix. The machines here are the world at its most cooperative, and knowing that is part of the lesson.
