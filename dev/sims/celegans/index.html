What am I looking at?

A nematode a millimetre long, magnified a few hundred times, on a dish of agar. Caenorhabditis elegans is the only animal whose entire nervous system has been mapped cell by cell: 302 neurons, and every synapse between them, published by White, Southgate, Thomson, and Brenner in 1986 after fifteen years of electron microscopy. That map, the connectome, is what runs this worm. Each of the 300 wired neurons here is a little voltage that leaks, sums what its neighbours send it through more than 2,000 chemical synapses and 500 electrical junctions, and passes the result on. Ninety five muscle cells along the body listen to the motor neurons and bend what they reach.

Nothing about the gait is drawn or scripted. The wave that carries the worm forward starts as a small alternation in the head, and each segment behind copies the bend it feels in front of itself, a trick the real animal plays with stretch receptors on its motor neurons. Slide a finger of food scent past the nose and the salt-sensing cells ASEL and ASER fire on rising and falling scent; their signal descends through the real wiring to the head muscles, and the worm steers up the gradient the way the animal does in the laboratory, by running straight when dinner is getting closer and casting about when it is not.

Touch it near the head and the touch cells ALM and AVM wake the command neurons AVA, AVD, and AVE, the same cells that flip the living worm into reverse. A long enough retreat ends in a deep ventral coil, the omega turn, and the worm sets off somewhere new. Every one of those decisions is visible as it happens in the grid of cells on the right: sensory neurons, interneurons, and motor neurons lighting up in the order the wiring dictates.

Things to try

  • Drop one meal of food far from the worm, turn the scent down low, and watch how long the search takes. Then turn the scent up and watch the path straighten.
  • Build a corridor of walls with food at the end. The worm cannot see; it feels its way, and corners genuinely cost it time.
  • Poke the head, then the tail. The head poke reverses it; the tail poke urges it forward. Two different touch circuits, and you can watch them fire.
  • Ring the worm with posts and see it thread between them. Nothing tells it where the gaps are except its own body bending.

Limits of the model

The wiring is the animal's own, but the physiology on top of it is simplified. Real C. elegans neurons carry graded potentials with rich channel dynamics; here each is a single leaky voltage with one gain, and synapse strength is the anatomical synapse count, signed by transmitter. The head's rhythm generator is not fully understood in the living animal and is stood in for by a small oscillator between the real head motor neurons. The stretch response of the motor neurons is modelled as an on-off switch with memory, following Boyle, Berri, and Cohen (2012), and the wavelength of the crawl is not written anywhere: it comes out of how slowly a bend develops against the agar. Muscle chemistry, egg laying, feeding, and the pharynx are absent, and the dish is two dimensional. The worm on your screen is a hypothesis about how this nervous system produces behaviour, not a copy of the animal; where it behaves like the real one, that is the wiring doing the work.
