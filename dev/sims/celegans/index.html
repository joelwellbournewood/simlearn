What am I looking at?

This is a simulation of the nervous system and the body of Caenorhabditis elegans, a soil nematode a millimetre long, running live in your browser. Three hundred nerve cells are wired to each other exactly as they are in the real animal. They drive ninety-five muscle cells. The muscles bend a physical body, the body pushes against a two-dimensional dish of agar, and the track it leaves is the result. Nothing about the animal's movement is drawn or scripted; it is whatever that chain of cells produces from one moment to the next.

The animal itself is real and ordinary. Turn over a rotting apple in a compost heap and you are holding thousands of them: transparent, grown from exactly 959 cells, living in the film of water between grains of soil and eating bacteria. In 1963 Sydney Brenner chose it as the animal biology would learn to read all the way through. By 1986 his group had cut an adult into thousands of electron-microscope sections and drawn all 302 of its neurons and every connection between them — a complete wiring diagram of a nervous system, which we still have for no other animal. That diagram is the circuit on this page.

Each neuron here holds a single voltage. It sums what arrives through some three and a half thousand chemical synapses and eleven hundred electrical junctions, leaks the charge away, and passes on what is left. At the end of the chain the motor neurons pull on muscle cells; a muscle that shortens bends its stretch of body; a bent body meets more drag on one side than the other and slides.

The crawl comes out of that. The head begins a small alternating bend, each segment behind it copies the bend it feels ahead of it a moment later, and a wave travels from head to tail that pushes the animal forward. Slide Medium from agar toward water and the stroke lengthens and quickens on its own, with no neuron changed — the same switch a real worm makes when it is lifted into a drop of water and starts to swim.

The dish holds the animal, its food and any walls you add. Food is a lawn of bacterial particles eaten one at a time, and an eaten particle stops smelling. A patch spreads its smell outward through the agar; walls and posts are solid and block both the body and the smell. The readouts open the animal up:

  • Neurons — all 300 cells as a grid, each one brightening with its own activity: sensory cells yellow, interneurons teal, motor neurons orange. Hover for what a cell does, click to pin it, then silence it and watch what the animal can no longer do. The grid behaves like a spreadsheet: drag a box across it, or shift-click, to take out a whole block of cells at once.
  • Head ganglia — the nerve ring, where most of the wiring sits, drawn as the graph it is. Green edges excite, pink edges inhibit, blue edges are electrical junctions that pass current both ways.
  • Muscles — the 95 body wall muscle cells in their four quadrants, with the wave crossing them.
  • Scent — the smell landscape as the animal meets it, and where its nose sits in it.
  • Signals — the last 24 seconds of this animal's life. The coloured strip along the top is what it was doing; the traces below are the cells that decided it, each labelled with its name and its job.

Two of those cells carry most of the behaviour. AVB holds the wave travelling head to tail; AVA takes the body off AVB and sends the wave the other way. Each suppresses the other, so the animal commits to one at a time, and a reversal followed by a sharp turn is how a worm changes its mind about where dinner is. Names like these are the anatomist's shorthand from the 1986 diagram — AVB is the class B interneuron of the anterior ganglion running down the ventral nerve cord, AVA the class A — and every name that appears on a readout is spelled out under The cell names on the readouts below.

Things to try

  • Pin a cell in the Neurons grid and silence it. Without ASE (the amphid taste cells) the animal crawls normally and stops finding food — it ate 0.09 of a patch in four minutes against 0.93 intact. Without the DB motor neurons that drive the dorsal muscles forward, the wave breaks up. Without AWC, the smell cells, almost nothing changes, because taste is doing the work in this dish. Drag a box across the grid to select many cells at once, shift-click to stretch the box, and silence the lot. Restore all brings them back.
  • Poke it near the head, then near the tail. One backs it up and spins it round; the other makes it sprint away. Watch the two command traces trade places on Signals.
  • Poke the same animal four times in twenty seconds. The reversals get shorter each time. Leave it a minute and it recovers.
  • Drop a wide patch of food in its path. It falls to about half speed and stays — which is why a worm that finds food keeps it.
  • Let it eat, then erase the patch. It searches tightly for a few minutes, then gives up and disperses in long straight runs.
  • Slide the medium from agar to water and watch the same circuit change gait.
  • Run Race at 10×: several animals with identical nervous systems, one meal, four gates.
  • Put Eight strangers on Groupers, then on Loners, and leave it running for two minutes.
  • Leave one animal alone with no food. It slides into pauses and slow crawling, then rouses itself and roams again.

The cell names on the readouts

Every neuron in this animal has a name of two or three capital letters, sometimes with a number for its place along the body and a final L, R, D or V for the left, right, dorsal or ventral member of a group. The letters are not arbitrary. They were assigned as the cells were identified in the electron micrographs, and each one encodes something the anatomist could see: which sense organ the cell belongs to, the shape of its sensory ending, which ganglion its cell body sits in, which muscles it drives. The last letter is usually just a serial label — A, B, C — distinguishing members of a family that otherwise look alike. Read that way the names are a compressed description of the anatomy, which is why the field has never replaced them.

The families. A… at the front of a sensory name means the amphid, the pair of open sensory pores on either side of the nose that most of the worm's chemistry arrives through. The second letter is the shape of the nerve ending inside it: AS… a single ciliated ending, AD… a dual one, AW… a wing-shaped sheet wrapped in the support cell, AF… a finger-like spray of microvilli. AI… is an amphid interneuron, one layer behind those sensors. RI… and RM… are ring interneurons and ring motor neurons, cells of the nerve ring, the brain-like bundle around the throat. AV…, PV… and DV… are ventral cord cells whose bodies sit in the anterior, posterior or dorsorectal ganglion. IL and OL are the inner and outer labial sensors ringing the mouth, PH… the phasmids, the amphids' small counterpart in the tail. Among the motor neurons of the cord the first letter is the muscle side the cell drives — D dorsal, V ventral — and the second is the class: A for the ones that run the animal backwards, B forwards, D for the inhibitory pair-partners that relax the opposite side.

The names this page puts on screen.

  • AVB — anterior ganglion, ventral cord interneuron, class B. The forward command pair. While it leads, the wave runs head to tail and the animal goes forward.
  • AVA — anterior ganglion, ventral cord interneuron, class A. The reverse command pair. While it leads, the wave runs tail to head.
  • AVD, AVE — the same family, feeding the reversal circuit; AVE's output is restricted to the front of the cord, so it reverses the neck first.
  • PVC — posterior ganglion, ventral cord interneuron, class C. Runs the length of the animal from the tail and drives the forward motor neurons alongside AVB.
  • ASE — amphid, single ciliated ending, class E. The taste cells of the nose: salt, and the soluble part of a bacterial lawn. They report how fast the concentration is changing, not how strong it is.
  • AWC — amphid, wing cell, class C. Smells volatile attractants drifting off bacteria, and fires when the smell falls away.
  • AWA, AWB — the other two wing cells: one more attractant channel, one for odours the worm avoids.
  • ASH — amphid, single ciliated ending, class H. The nose's alarm cell — harsh touch, bitter chemicals, high osmolarity — and the trigger for backing away.
  • ASK, ASI, ASJ, ASG — more amphid single-ending cells, reading pheromone, food quality and the crowding signals that decide whether a larva grows up or waits.
  • ADL, ADF — amphid, dual ciliated ending. ADL smells the pheromone of other worms; ADF makes serotonin in response to food.
  • AFD — amphid, finger cell. The thermometer: it remembers the temperature the animal grew up at.
  • AIY, AIB, AIZ, AIA — amphid interneurons. The first layer behind the nose, where a smell becomes a decision to keep going or turn.
  • FLP, OLQ, IL1 — head mechanosensors. OLQ is an outer labial, quadrant cell and IL1 an inner labial cell, both in the ring of sensilla around the mouth; FLP is a free ciliated ending in the head with no support cell of its own and no published expansion of its letters. Together they report a knock on the nose.
  • ALM, AVM, PLM, PVM — anterior/posterior, lateral/ventral, microtubule cell. The gentle touch receptors, named for the unusually thick 15-protofilament microtubules that fill them. ALM and AVM report a prod near the head, PLM near the tail.
  • CEP, ADE, PDE — cephalic, anterior deirid, posterior deirid: the dopamine cells, set in sensilla along the head and body, which feel bacteria dragging under the animal.
  • NSM — neurosecretory motor neuron, in the pharynx. It holds serotonin and reports food actually arriving in the throat.
  • HSN — hermaphrodite-specific neuron, present only in hermaphrodites, which is what it is named for. It drives egg laying and adds serotonin.
  • RIM — ring motor neuron, class M. The tyramine cell: it quiets the head and holds a reversal open during an escape.
  • RIA, RIB, RIV, RIS — ring interneurons. RIA is the steering integrator, RIB tracks the forward state, RIV bends the animal into a turn, RIS puts it to sleep.
  • RMD, RME, SMD, SMB, SAA — ring and sublateral motor neurons of the head and neck. RMD and RME set head bend; SMD and SMB send a process back along the body under the skin; SAA reports how far the head is bent.
  • RMG — the hub cell. The pheromone and oxygen sensors are all wired into it electrically, which is where social behaviour comes from.
  • URX, AQR, PQR — the oxygen sensors. URX is a head cell with an ending in the nose; AQR and PQR have no sense organ at all and taste the animal's own body fluid. Their A and P are where the cell body ends up, and their Q is the Q neuroblast they are born from — the same division, front and back.
  • BAG — the carbon dioxide sensor, named for the flattened bag-shaped ending on its nerve.
  • DB, VB — dorsal/ventral muscle, class B: the cord motor neurons that drive the forward wave, numbered from head to tail.
  • DA, VA — class A: the same arrangement for backing up.
  • DD, VD — class D: the inhibitory ones. They relax the far side of the body so a bend can actually form.
  • AS — class A, single: a dorsal motor neuron with no ventral partner.
  • DVA — dorsorectal ganglion, ventral cord, class A. It senses how far the body itself is bent.
  • PHA, PHB — phasmid chemosensors in the tail.

A few names resist this. FLP and BAG have no expansion anyone published; they are labels that stuck. The trailing letters of the command interneurons are alphabetical, not mnemonic — AVA is not "A for backward". Where the letters mean nothing, saying so is more useful than inventing a story.

Silencing a cell

Most of what is known about this nervous system was learned by killing one named cell with a laser beam and watching what the animal could no longer do (Chalfie, Sulston, White, Southgate, Thomson and Brenner 1985). The Neurons panel does the same thing: pin a cell and the button under the grid silences its whole class, left and right member together, because killing one of a pair usually changes nothing visible. A silenced cell holds no voltage and releases nothing, so every cell downstream of it simply loses that input; it shows in the grid as a crossed-out square. Nothing else about the animal is touched, which is the point — whatever changes on the dish is what that class was contributing.

The chain from cell to movement

Sense organs write current into real cells, the connectome carries it, motor neurons pull on muscle cells, and a body with drag on it bends and travels. Everything on screen is the far end of that chain. A reversal is AVA winning against AVB. Steering is ASE and AWC firing as the smell rises and falls, biasing which way the head casts. Crawling is stretch feedback running down the body. Silence the sensory cells and the animal still moves but stops finding food; break the wiring and none of it survives.

Smell, food and slowing down

Each bacterial particle is its own scent source with a long diffusion plume around it. The nose answers to fractional change in concentration rather than its absolute level (Larsch et al. 2015), which is what lets the animal climb a faint tail of smell from across the dish. Walls cast a scent shadow, so inside a corridor the gradient points at the opening rather than straight through the plastic. On the lawn, the dopamine cells CEP, ADE and PDE feel bacteria under the body and cut the crawl to roughly half speed — the basal slowing response of Sawin, Ranganathan and Horvitz (2000) — and the pharyngeal cell NSM deepens it while the animal is actually swallowing. NSM is an enteric sensor: it detects food arriving in the throat within a second or two of the first pump, through acid-sensing channels on its own membrane (Rhoades et al. 2019), so its serotonin signal on the Signals chart is an event and not a level — a spike as a meal starts, falling back to a plateau while the animal keeps eating, gone within seconds of the food running out. Slowing on food is the reason a worm that stumbles onto a meal is still on it a minute later.

What a prod does

Poke near the head and the touch receptors ALM and AVM, with the nose sensors ASH and FLP, hand the animal to AVA for a second or two of backing that finishes in a sharp turn. Poke near the tail and PLM pushes AVB instead: the worm runs forward about 1.4× faster. That is the escape circuit of Chalfie and Sulston (1981), with RIM's tyramine quieting the head during the retreat. It habituates too — each prod costs about a quarter of the response, and a minute of quiet restores it (Rankin & Broster 1992).

Many worms, one gene

Up to eight animals share the dish, each with its own nervous system, body and colour; click one to send every readout to it. They meet through the circuit. Every worm sheds ascaroside pheromone, its neighbours smell it with ADL and ASK, and those cells together with the oxygen sensors URX, AQR and PQR are gap-junctioned to one hub neuron, RMG. The gene npr-1 damps that hub. Loners are N2, the laboratory strain dug out of Bristol soil in 1963 and kept by worm labs ever since. Groupers are the same animal with npr-1 broken, which is what most worms caught in the wild are (de Bono & Bargmann 1998; Macosko et al. 2009). Nothing in the code tells them to gather: with five animals, the mean distance between them falls from 2.4 body lengths to between 0.5 and 1.0 in groupers, and rises to 3 or 4 in loners.

Why a wall is hard

There is no path-finding anywhere in this animal. A gradient gives a direction and nothing else: keep going while the smell rises, turn when it falls.

What makes a barrier interesting is that the smell has to get round it too. Odour reaches the nose by diffusing through the agar, and a wall is as solid to a molecule as it is to a worm, so the concentration at any point falls off with the length of the shortest open path back to the food — not with the straight line through the plastic. The dish computes that path for every point on the plate. Stand in a corridor and the strongest smell is genuinely toward the gap, not toward the wall, and a corner leaks nothing at all. Watch the Scent panel with the Maze preset and the shape of the answer is drawn there: two bright lobes joined through the openings.

That still does not hand the animal a route. It only means that following the smell uphill is a strategy that can work, and a worm has no more than that. Press it into the wall anyway and four things eventually free it: the nose reflex habituates, the smell sense adapts to a steady concentration until the direction fades, every reversal long enough to count ends in a turn that re-aims the body by a large angle, and sustained contact that is getting the animal nowhere finally reaches the escape cells. Escape is a race between a fading reflex and a lucky turn, which is why it looks like sudden insight after a minute of stupidity. Real worms in real mazes solve them the same way, by biased chance.

Measured against the living animal

On the agar setting the model crawls at 0.49 Hz with a wavelength of 0.79 body lengths and a nearly pure sine wave (waveform purity 0.84); the measured animal crawls at about 0.5 Hz and 0.65 lengths. Moved to water the same circuit swims at about 1.6 Hz, against roughly 2 Hz in the laboratory (Fang-Yen et al. 2010). Spontaneous reversals come about eight times a minute and last 1.4 to 1.6 seconds on average, within the one to two seconds seen in freely moving animals. Escape reversals reorient the body by about 115 degrees, against 140 to 180 in the animal, and the bends on agar are a little shallower than the real ones. Released far from a single food patch it reaches the patch in every trial, roughly twice as fast as the same animal with its chemosensory neurons silenced.

The sharper test is neural rather than behavioural. Randi, Sharma, Dvali and Leifer (2023) stimulated single neurons in living, immobilised animals and recorded which other cells answered, turning some twenty-three thousand pairs of cells into a measurement of what actually reaches what. Against that atlas the model's strength is where it should be: when the wiring diagram says one cell excites another, the model's prediction of a response is right about nine times in ten, and the cells this page puts on screen — the command interneurons, the cord motor neurons, the amphid sensors and the layer behind them — are exactly the ones whose signal flow the anatomy accounts for best. Those are short, dense, heavily synapsed paths, and they are the paths the behaviour on the dish is built out of.

The atlas also shows where the wiring diagram runs out, and it is worth being precise about what that gap is. Many cell pairs in the real animal influence each other with no synapse and no gap junction between them, because neurons release peptides that drift through the tissue and act on whatever carries the matching receptor. The map of which cells make which peptide and which cells can receive it now exists (Ripoll-Sanchez et al. 2023) and is in the model as a slow chemical layer. What does not exist, for any animal, is the sign and the strength: for most of the worm's hundred-odd peptide receptors nobody yet knows whether binding excites the cell or quiets it, how long the effect lasts, or how much peptide is around. Until that is measured, a wiring-based model of this nervous system will keep predicting the fast, synapse-carried half of the traffic well and the slow, diffuse half poorly. That is not a flaw in the connectome; it is the frontier of the subject.

Limits of the model

The wiring is the animal's own; the physiology on top of it is simplified. Real C. elegans neurons carry graded potentials with rich channel dynamics, while each cell here is a single leaky voltage with one gain, and synapse strength is the anatomical synapse count, signed by the receptor expression of the target cell where Fenyves et al. (2020) make a confident prediction and by transmitter otherwise. Cells with no rhythm of their own — the sublateral motor neurons and the head cells outside the steering circuit — are held at a supporting muscle tone rather than allowed to drive the wave, which is an assumption anatomy alone cannot settle.

Three things are set by hand rather than grown from the circuit. The rhythm that starts the wave comes from a small oscillator between the real head motor neurons, because how the living animal generates that rhythm is still an open question. The slow timescales — roaming and dwelling bouts, the tight search that follows a lost meal — follow published measurements. And a handful of gains are fitted: dopamine's grip on the motor neurons, the strength of the social rules, the balance between shallow and deep turns.

Stretch feedback in the motor neurons is an on-off switch with memory, following Boyle, Berri and Cohen (2012), and the wavelength of the crawl is written nowhere: it falls out of how slowly a bend develops against the agar. The quick foraging flicks of the nose and the pace of the swim are set to match measurements. Behind NSM's fast signal the model carries a slow serotonin tone, built up from recent feeding over minutes, which biases how long roaming and dwelling bouts last (Flavell et al. 2013); the time constant of that tone is a guess, and it has no link to food density in the dish or to true time since the last meal. How long an animal will follow a barrier before giving up is fitted too. Muscle chemistry, egg laying, the pharynx and feeding itself are absent, and the dish is two-dimensional. The animal on your screen is a hypothesis about how this nervous system produces behaviour; where it behaves like the real one, the wiring is doing the work.
