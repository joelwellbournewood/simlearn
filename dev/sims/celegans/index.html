What am I looking at?

A nematode a millimetre long, magnified a few hundred times, on a dish of agar. Caenorhabditis elegans is the only animal whose entire nervous system has been mapped cell by cell: 302 neurons, and every synapse between them, published by White, Southgate, Thomson, and Brenner in 1986 after fifteen years of electron microscopy. That map has been redrawn ever since, and this worm runs on the modern one: the whole-animal reconstruction of Cook et al. (2019), with the transmitter each connection releases taken from the CRISPR reporter atlas of Wang et al. (2024), and the sign of each synapse, whether it excites or inhibits its target, predicted from the receptors the target cell actually expresses (Fenyves et al. 2020). Each of the 300 wired neurons here is a little voltage that leaks, sums what its neighbours send it through 3,541 signed chemical connections and 1,101 electrical junctions, and passes the result on. Ninety five muscle cells along the body listen through 956 neuromuscular junctions, and neighbouring muscle cells are electrically coupled to each other, exactly as the electron microscopy says they are. Underneath the wiring run two wireless networks. One carries the amines: 1,932 extrasynaptic channels along which dopamine, serotonin, tyramine, and octopamine drift between cells that share no synapse (Bentley et al. 2016). The other carries the neuropeptides: 31,397 connections inferred from which cells release each peptide and which cells express its receptor, taken from the neuropeptidergic connectome of Ripoll-Sanchez et al. (2023). In this model that second network acts slowly and, on balance, inhibits, which is what the living recordings demanded (see below).

Nothing about the gait is drawn or scripted. The wave that carries the worm forward starts as a small alternation in the head, and each segment behind copies the bend it feels in front of itself, a trick the real animal plays with stretch receptors on its motor neurons. Slide a finger of food scent past the nose and the salt-sensing cells ASEL and ASER fire on rising and falling scent; their signal descends through the real wiring to the head muscles, and the worm steers up the gradient the way the animal does in the laboratory: it runs straight when dinner is getting closer, casts about when it is not, and leans each swing of its head toward the side that smells better.

Touch it near the head and the touch cells ALM and AVM wake the command neurons AVA, AVD, and AVE, the same cells that flip the living worm into reverse. The worm also feels the world without your help: drive it nose first into a wall and the nose cells ASH, FLP, and OLQ fire and throw it into reverse, the response Kaplan and Horvitz mapped in 1993, and it fades if the stimulus goes on and on, the way the animal habituates. A brush on the side of the nose makes the head pull away instead, through the labial cells OLQ and IL1. And while it backs up, the tyramine cell RIM quiets the head the way the real escape response does, so the retreat is a clean slide rather than a wobble, and the reversal runs long enough to matter. Any retreat that clears about a third of a second ends in a turn, and which kind depends on what caused it: a retreat that started because the nose actually touched something ends in the full deep ventral coil, the head sweeping back to meet the tail, that ethologists have called the omega turn since Croll first named it in 1975. A retreat with no such contact, the kind a pirouette or a spontaneous pause triggers on its own, ends in the shallower upsilon turn instead: the same ventral pull at roughly forty percent strength and well under half the duration, enough to swing the heading without folding the body onto itself. Real worms show both kinds from both causes; this model draws the line at the trigger as a simplification (a model assumption), because it is the split that keeps a poke reliably dramatic while letting the worm's own spontaneous reorientations read as searching rather than constant emergencies. Every one of those decisions is visible as it happens in the panel on the right, five windows on the same machine that you can mix and match: the grid of all 300 neurons lighting up in the order the wiring dictates; a firing map that places every neuron at its true anatomical position in the body it is driving, soma coordinates from the wormneuroatlas anatomical atlas projected on the plane the crawl happens in; the muscle map, the 24 dorsal and ventral muscle pairs painted on the live outline of the body so you can see which side of which bend is pulling; the scent field with its gradient arrow and the gaps the worm has grazed; and a chart recorder scrolling the last half minute of the command neurons and the slow chemistry. The panel expands if you want a bigger look.

Food here is not a disc, it is a lawn. Dropping a meal scatters dozens of individual bacterial particles, dense at the centre and thinning outward, and every particle is its own source of scent: the worm eats them one at a time, and where it grazes, both the food and its smell disappear. Around each patch the agar carries a long shallow plume, the diffusion tail a real patch grows, and the sensing follows the Weber law: ASEL and ASER respond to the fractional change in concentration rather than the absolute one (Larsch et al. 2015), which is why the worm can steer up the faint outer tail of a plume from across the dish and still resolve the steep rim of the patch itself.

Food changes the worm's mind chemically. The dopamine cells CEP, ADE, and PDE are mechanosensors that feel the texture of bacteria under the body, and while they fire, their dopamine slows the crawl: the basal slowing response of Sawin, Ranganathan, and Horvitz (2000), the worm's way of staying where dinner is. While it actually eats, the serotonin cell NSM tastes food in the throat and deepens the slowdown. Drop a wide patch of food in its path and you can watch all of it happen: CEP lights, the stride stretches out, and the tracks change from cruising to dwelling.

The dish itself is a physics experiment. On agar the worm cuts a groove and crawls slow and tight; slide the medium toward water and the very same circuit swims, faster and longer waved, with nothing changed but the drag on its body. That continuous gait change is exactly what Berri (2009) and Fang-Yen (2010) measured by thickening the fluid around real worms.

Left running, the worm does not crawl forever. Cermak, Yu, Clark, Huang, Baskoylu, and Flavell (2020) tracked 30 well-fed worms for six hours apiece and fit a nine-state hidden Markov model to their posture: one roaming state, fast and nearly straight, lasting anywhere from tens of seconds to several minutes, and eight distinct dwelling sub-modes averaging about ten seconds each, most of them almost completely still, one a slow steady crawl, one dominated by backing up, one full of wide head and neck sweeps. This model now runs that same structure: it alternates a roaming bout, thirty seconds to two and a half minutes of exactly the validated crawl described below, with a dwelling epoch built from chained ten-second sub-bouts in the paper's own proportions: five in eight paused, one in eight a slow crawl, one in eight repeated reversals, one in eight broad head casting. The sub-mode identities and their roughly ten-second timing come straight from the paper; the paper's own numeric speeds live only in its figures, not its text, so the exact slow-down and reversal-rate values inside each sub-mode are tagged as model assumptions, tuned to look and move like the states the paper describes rather than fitted to numbers we could read off it.

What used to pick roam or dwell was a plain coin flip. It now runs on two separable signals that are deliberately built to look and behave differently, because the underlying cells are different. Dopamine, from CEP, ADE, and PDE, is fast: those cells are mechanosensors of bacterial texture and report within a second or two of the body touching food (Sawin et al. 2000). Serotonin is modeled as two things at once, matching the two timescales in the literature: a fast pumping-linked signal from NSM, and a much slower "satiety tone" that integrates feeding over roughly a minute and, unlike a single pump, survives brief gaps off food. That slow tone is what the roam and dwell timers now read: a well-fed worm (high tone) draws longer dwelling bouts and shorter roaming bouts, and a food-deprived one draws the reverse, which is the direction Flavell, Pokala, Macosko, Albrecht, Larsch, and Bargmann (2013) found when they showed serotonin (through the MOD-1 receptor) promotes dwelling and its absence (tph-1 mutants) shifts time toward roaming, a result Cermak et al. (2020) independently reproduced in the same posture-HMM data used above. Neither paper publishes the exact time constant of that slow tone, so its value (about ninety seconds) is tagged as a model assumption; the direction of the effect is not. A second, smaller but real effect closes out the old "poking does nothing" gap: mechanical stimulation measurably suppresses pharyngeal pumping in adult worms within a pathway Keane and Avery (2003) traced to the glutamate receptors AVR-14 and AVR-15, so a poke now visibly dips the serotonin trace for a few seconds and recovers, rather than sitting inert next to a touch that clearly did something to every other signal on screen. Dopamine, by contrast, is not known to be touch-linked at all; CEP, ADE, and PDE are food-texture sensors, not the touch-receptor neurons ALM, AVM, and PLM that this model's escape reflex already uses, so leaving dopamine unmoved by a poke is not an oversight, it is the same separation of labor the real animal keeps.

Two changes this build make the search itself look purposeful rather than twitchy. The first is in how a falling odour concentration triggers a pirouette. The worm's nose sweeps side to side with every undulation, so the raw sensory derivative swings negative twice a second whatever direction the animal is actually heading; feeding that straight into the reversal command made it stutter, reversing about eleven times a minute near food, two thirds of those gaps under two seconds, where real animals' inter-reversal intervals form a smooth lognormal with a median around thirteen seconds (Zhao, Khare, Feng, Butler and Lockery 2003). The fix is to commit on evidence rather than on the instant: the signed derivative is now integrated over about a second before it can flip the command, which cancels the sweep (it is symmetric, so it averages away) while a genuine descent, which is one-sided, survives intact; on top of that a large abrupt drop still fires immediately, so the step stimuli of Pierce-Shimomura, Morse and Lockery (1999) work as they should. Measured over five independent six-trial batteries, the worm now finds a food patch it cannot see in about five trials in six, against fewer than three in six before, and its closest approach improves roughly threefold. Counterintuitively, being slower to panic makes it a much better navigator.

The second is what happens when the food runs out. Gray, Hill and Bargmann (2005) and Hills, Brockie and Maricq (2004) showed that a worm taken off food does not simply wander away: for roughly the first fifteen minutes it performs a local, area-restricted search, reversing and turning far more often than usual and staying almost on the spot, and only then switches to global search, dispersing in long, nearly straight runs. The local phase needs dopamine, which is why animals that cannot make it disperse straight away, so here it is gated on a decaying memory of recent feeding rather than on a bare clock. Measured on this model: in the first five minutes after the patch is removed the worm reverses about ten times a minute and travels a net nine body-lengths, and twenty minutes later it reverses three times a minute and covers twice the ground in a straighter line; a worm that never found food at all shows neither phase. The chemotaxis drive is deliberately left at full strength throughout, so a disperser that runs into a new gradient still turns and climbs it.

This build goes after the model's most stubborn number: how often the worm backs up. Three separate things were driving reversals that should not have been. First, the OFF response of the odour-sensing cells never adapted, so a worm eating its way through a patch sat in a permanently falling concentration and kept re-firing the reversal command neuron AVA; real AWC responses adapt within seconds (Chalasani et al. 2007). Second, the area-restricted search drive — the turn-heavy hunt a worm performs once it has lost its food (Gray et al. 2005; Hills et al. 2004) — was running at full strength while the animal was still sitting on the lawn, firing a reversal attempt every few seconds. Third, the roam/dwell arousal level was scaling the forward command cell AVB but not the phasic drive onto AVA, so a resting worm's command circuit was quietly biased into reverse. With all three fixed the command circuit spends 52% of its time in reverse near food instead of 76%, the median gap between reversals grows by about half, and the worm is better at finding a patch it cannot see. The headline rate — still around twelve reversals a minute, against a real median interval near 13 s (Zhao et al. 2003) — barely moved, and the honest reading is that the rest of the excess is below the command circuit: the body changes direction even while the command neurons hold their state. That is the next thing to chase, and it stays on the scorecard as a failure rather than being quietly rounded off.

Things to try

  • Drop one meal of food far from the worm, turn the scent down low, and watch how long the search takes. Then turn the scent up and watch the path straighten.
  • Build a corridor of walls with food at the end. The worm cannot see; it feels its way, and corners genuinely cost it time.
  • Poke the head, then the tail. The head poke reverses it; the tail poke urges it forward. Two different touch circuits, and you can watch them fire.
  • Ring the worm with posts and see it thread between them. Nothing tells it where the gaps are except its own body bending.
  • Watch it reorient on its own for a minute, then poke it once: the unprompted turns stay partial (upsilon, amber on the state strip), the poked one goes all the way to a full omega (gold). Same circuit, different depth, because only one of them started with real contact.
  • Build a dead end corner and let it drive in. Watch ASH and FLP light up at the moment of the bump, the reversal, the deep turn, and the exit. Then trap it again and again and see the response wear down.
  • Watch the scent map in the right-hand panel while the worm climbs a plume: the gold arrow at the nose points up the gradient, and the map thins out wherever it has grazed.
  • Turn on the Firing map. The small body diagram never bends (it is a fixed reference, the moving worm is already right there on the dish); the panel below it expands the head into the nerve ring and head ganglia, spread out and wired with their real synapses so cells that overlap up top can be told apart. Click any neuron, in the grid or on either body diagram, and the same cell is ringed in all three views at once.
  • Turn on Signals and poke the nose: AVB hands over to AVA at the instant of the reversal, the state strip above the traces turns orange for the reversal, then gold for the omega turn it becomes, and the serotonin trace dips for a few seconds and recovers, the pumping-suppression reflex Keane and Avery described.
  • Drop a meal and let the worm settle on it: it grazes the lawn particle by particle, and in the Scent view the map dims exactly where it has eaten.
  • Slide the medium from agar to water and watch the stroke lengthen and quicken without a single neuron changing.
  • Lay a broad patch of food and watch the worm slow down on it. The dopamine cells CEP, ADE, and PDE light while the body is over food, and NSM joins them while it eats. That slowdown is why the chemotaxis works: finding food is only half the job, staying on it is the other half.
  • Leave it alone for a couple of minutes and just watch: it will downshift out of its fast straight run into a still pause, a slow crawl, or a flurry of backing up, then pick a new heading and roam again, unprompted.
  • Feed it, then delete the patch and watch the clock: the first few minutes are a tight, turn-heavy local search around where dinner used to be, and after a quarter of an hour it gives up and disperses in long straight runs (Gray et al. 2005).
  • Keep it on a food patch for a few minutes, then pull it off: the Signals serotonin trace climbs slowly while it feeds and decays slowly once it leaves, and dwelling bouts stretch on the patch and roaming bouts stretch off it, the exploration/exploitation trade-off of Flavell et al. (2013).

How well does it match the living animal?

Two kinds of measurement back the worm on your screen. The first is kinematic. On the agar setting it crawls at 0.49 Hz with a wavelength of 0.79 body lengths and a nearly pure sine wave (waveform purity 0.84); the measured animal crawls at about 0.5 Hz and 0.65 lengths. Slide the medium to water and the same circuit swims at about 1.6 Hz, against roughly 2 Hz in the laboratory (Fang-Yen et al. 2010). Its escape reversals back it up and reorient it by about 115 degrees on average, versus 140 to 180 in the animal; its bends on agar are somewhat shallower than the real ones. Released far from a single food patch, it found the patch in 4 of 4 trials with a working nose, roughly twice as fast as the same worm with its chemosensory neurons silenced.

The second is neural, and it is the hardest test available. Randi, Sharma, Dvali, and Leifer (2023) optogenetically stimulated single neurons in living immobilized worms and recorded who answered: a functional atlas of 23,000 or so stimulus and response pairs. We ran the same protocol on this model, stimulating each of the 173 atlas neurons and reading out every other cell. Anatomy alone hit a ceiling here: the wired model caught 9 of every 10 excitatory responses but barely 1 in 5 inhibitory ones, and when we traced the misses, 132 of the 149 significant inhibitory pairs turned out to have no synapse or gap junction between them at all. Randi and colleagues' own headline finding is exactly that: signal flow in the real worm routinely disagrees with anatomy because neuropeptides signal between cells that share no synapse. So we added the missing network, the neuropeptidergic connectome above, as a slow inhibitory layer with a single fitted gain, and re-ran the whole atlas. The result, cross-checked on held-out stimulation targets so the model is not graded on its own homework: the model now tells responding pairs from silent ones measurably better (AUC 0.57 to 0.62), the correlation across all 23,901 pairs rises from 0.06 to 0.09, and inhibitory detection nearly doubles to 37 percent, while on the significant pairs the amplitude ranking stays at rho = 0.16, still about what anatomy predicts of itself (rho = 0.14). The known extrasynaptic amine network (Bentley et al. 2016) was tested the same way and carries no predictive signal for these fast responses, so it stays in the model as slow chemistry only. The remaining gap is real physiology nobody has mapped at this level: which of the several dozen peptide receptors excites and which inhibits, cell by cell.

Limits of the model

The wiring is the animal's own, but the physiology on top of it is simplified. Real C. elegans neurons carry graded potentials with rich channel dynamics; here each is a single leaky voltage with one gain, and synapse strength is the anatomical synapse count, signed by the receptor expression of the target cell where Fenyves et al. (2020) make a confident prediction and by transmitter otherwise. Cells with no known rhythm of their own, the sublateral motor neurons and the head cells outside the steering circuit, are held to a supporting muscle tone rather than allowed to drive the wave, which is an assumption the anatomy alone cannot settle. The head's rhythm generator is not fully understood in the living animal and is stood in for by a small oscillator between the real head motor neurons. The stretch response of the motor neurons is modelled as an on-off switch with memory, following Boyle, Berri, and Cohen (2012), and the wavelength of the crawl is not written anywhere: it comes out of how slowly a bend develops against the agar. The quick foraging flicks of the nose tip and the pace of the swim rhythm in thin fluid are set by hand to match the measured behaviour rather than emerging from the wiring, and both are tagged as assumptions in the code. The switch between roaming and dwelling is now biased by a slow serotonin tone built from recent feeding (Flavell et al. 2013), but it still has no link to food density in the environment or true time-since-last-meal physiology, and the size of the bias (the multiplier applied to bout length) is a tuned model assumption, not a fitted number; the sub-mode identities and their rough ten-second timing are matched to the paper, the precise speed and reversal-rate inside each are not. Muscle chemistry, egg laying, feeding, and the pharynx are absent, and the dish is two dimensional. The worm on your screen is a hypothesis about how this nervous system produces behaviour, not a copy of the animal; where it behaves like the real one, that is the wiring doing the work.
