What am I looking at?

A nematode a millimetre long, magnified a few hundred times, on a dish of agar. Caenorhabditis elegans is the only animal whose entire nervous system has been mapped cell by cell: 302 neurons, and every synapse between them, published by White, Southgate, Thomson, and Brenner in 1986 after fifteen years of electron microscopy. That map has been redrawn ever since, and this worm runs on the modern one: the whole-animal reconstruction of Cook et al. (2019), with the transmitter each connection releases taken from the CRISPR reporter atlas of Wang et al. (2024), and the sign of each synapse, whether it excites or inhibits its target, predicted from the receptors the target cell actually expresses (Fenyves et al. 2020). Each of the 300 wired neurons here is a little voltage that leaks, sums what its neighbours send it through 3,541 signed chemical connections and 1,101 electrical junctions, and passes the result on. Ninety five muscle cells along the body listen through 956 neuromuscular junctions, and neighbouring muscle cells are electrically coupled to each other, exactly as the electron microscopy says they are. Underneath the wiring run two wireless networks. One carries the amines: 1,932 extrasynaptic channels along which dopamine, serotonin, tyramine, and octopamine drift between cells that share no synapse (Bentley et al. 2016). The other carries the neuropeptides: 31,397 connections inferred from which cells release each peptide and which cells express its receptor, taken from the neuropeptidergic connectome of Ripoll-Sanchez et al. (2023). In this model that second network acts slowly and, on balance, inhibits, which is what the living recordings demanded (see below).

Nothing about the gait is drawn or scripted. The wave that carries the worm forward starts as a small alternation in the head, and each segment behind copies the bend it feels in front of itself, a trick the real animal plays with stretch receptors on its motor neurons. Slide a finger of food scent past the nose and the salt-sensing cells ASEL and ASER fire on rising and falling scent; their signal descends through the real wiring to the head muscles, and the worm steers up the gradient the way the animal does in the laboratory: it runs straight when dinner is getting closer, casts about when it is not, and leans each swing of its head toward the side that smells better.

Touch it near the head and the touch cells ALM and AVM wake the command neurons AVA, AVD, and AVE, the same cells that flip the living worm into reverse. The worm also feels the world without your help: drive it nose first into a wall and the nose cells ASH, FLP, and OLQ fire and throw it into reverse, the response Kaplan and Horvitz mapped in 1993, and it fades if the stimulus goes on and on, the way the animal habituates. A brush on the side of the nose makes the head pull away instead, through the labial cells OLQ and IL1. And while it backs up, the tyramine cell RIM quiets the head the way the real escape response does, so the retreat is a clean slide rather than a wobble, and the reversal runs long enough to matter. A long enough retreat ends in a deep ventral coil, the omega turn, and the worm sets off somewhere new. Every one of those decisions is visible as it happens in the panel on the right, three windows stacked on the same machine: the grid of all 300 neurons lighting up in the order the wiring dictates, the 24 rows of dorsal and ventral muscle drive, and the scent field exactly as the nose smells it.

Food here is not a disc, it is a lawn. Dropping a meal scatters dozens of individual bacterial particles, dense at the centre and thinning outward, and every particle is its own source of scent: the worm eats them one at a time, and where it grazes, both the food and its smell disappear. Around each patch the agar carries a long shallow plume, the diffusion tail a real patch grows, and the sensing follows the Weber law: ASEL and ASER respond to the fractional change in concentration rather than the absolute one (Larsch et al. 2015), which is why the worm can steer up the faint outer tail of a plume from across the dish and still resolve the steep rim of the patch itself.

Food changes the worm's mind chemically. The dopamine cells CEP, ADE, and PDE are mechanosensors that feel the texture of bacteria under the body, and while they fire, their dopamine slows the crawl: the basal slowing response of Sawin, Ranganathan, and Horvitz (2000), the worm's way of staying where dinner is. While it actually eats, the serotonin cell NSM tastes food in the throat and deepens the slowdown. Drop a wide patch of food in its path and you can watch all of it happen: CEP lights, the stride stretches out, and the tracks change from cruising to dwelling.

The dish itself is a physics experiment. On agar the worm cuts a groove and crawls slow and tight; slide the medium toward water and the very same circuit swims, faster and longer waved, with nothing changed but the drag on its body. That continuous gait change is exactly what Berri (2009) and Fang-Yen (2010) measured by thickening the fluid around real worms.

Things to try

  • Drop one meal of food far from the worm, turn the scent down low, and watch how long the search takes. Then turn the scent up and watch the path straighten.
  • Build a corridor of walls with food at the end. The worm cannot see; it feels its way, and corners genuinely cost it time.
  • Poke the head, then the tail. The head poke reverses it; the tail poke urges it forward. Two different touch circuits, and you can watch them fire.
  • Ring the worm with posts and see it thread between them. Nothing tells it where the gaps are except its own body bending.
  • Build a dead end corner and let it drive in. Watch ASH and FLP light up at the moment of the bump, the reversal, the deep turn, and the exit. Then trap it again and again and see the response wear down.
  • Watch the scent map in the right-hand panel while the worm climbs a plume, and watch the same map thin out wherever it has grazed.
  • Drop a meal and let the worm settle on it: it grazes the lawn particle by particle, and in the Scent view the map dims exactly where it has eaten.
  • Slide the medium from agar to water and watch the stroke lengthen and quicken without a single neuron changing.
  • Lay a broad patch of food and watch the worm slow down on it. The dopamine cells CEP, ADE, and PDE light while the body is over food, and NSM joins them while it eats. That slowdown is why the chemotaxis works: finding food is only half the job, staying on it is the other half.

How well does it match the living animal?

Two kinds of measurement back the worm on your screen. The first is kinematic. On the agar setting it crawls at 0.49 Hz with a wavelength of 0.79 body lengths and a nearly pure sine wave (waveform purity 0.84); the measured animal crawls at about 0.5 Hz and 0.65 lengths. Slide the medium to water and the same circuit swims at about 1.6 Hz, against roughly 2 Hz in the laboratory (Fang-Yen et al. 2010). Its escape reversals back it up and reorient it by about 115 degrees on average, versus 140 to 180 in the animal; its bends on agar are somewhat shallower than the real ones. Released far from a single food patch, it found the patch in 4 of 4 trials with a working nose, roughly twice as fast as the same worm with its chemosensory neurons silenced.

The second is neural, and it is the hardest test available. Randi, Sharma, Dvali, and Leifer (2023) optogenetically stimulated single neurons in living immobilized worms and recorded who answered: a functional atlas of 23,000 or so stimulus and response pairs. We ran the same protocol on this model, stimulating each of the 173 atlas neurons and reading out every other cell. Anatomy alone hit a ceiling here: the wired model caught 9 of every 10 excitatory responses but barely 1 in 5 inhibitory ones, and when we traced the misses, 132 of the 149 significant inhibitory pairs turned out to have no synapse or gap junction between them at all. Randi and colleagues' own headline finding is exactly that: signal flow in the real worm routinely disagrees with anatomy because neuropeptides signal between cells that share no synapse. So we added the missing network, the neuropeptidergic connectome above, as a slow inhibitory layer with a single fitted gain, and re-ran the whole atlas. The result, cross-checked on held-out stimulation targets so the model is not graded on its own homework: the model now tells responding pairs from silent ones measurably better (AUC 0.57 to 0.62), the correlation across all 23,901 pairs rises from 0.06 to 0.09, and inhibitory detection nearly doubles to 37 percent, while on the significant pairs the amplitude ranking stays at rho = 0.16, still about what anatomy predicts of itself (rho = 0.14). The known extrasynaptic amine network (Bentley et al. 2016) was tested the same way and carries no predictive signal for these fast responses, so it stays in the model as slow chemistry only. The remaining gap is real physiology nobody has mapped at this level: which of the several dozen peptide receptors excites and which inhibits, cell by cell.

Limits of the model

The wiring is the animal's own, but the physiology on top of it is simplified. Real C. elegans neurons carry graded potentials with rich channel dynamics; here each is a single leaky voltage with one gain, and synapse strength is the anatomical synapse count, signed by the receptor expression of the target cell where Fenyves et al. (2020) make a confident prediction and by transmitter otherwise. Cells with no known rhythm of their own, the sublateral motor neurons and the head cells outside the steering circuit, are held to a supporting muscle tone rather than allowed to drive the wave, which is an assumption the anatomy alone cannot settle. The head's rhythm generator is not fully understood in the living animal and is stood in for by a small oscillator between the real head motor neurons. The stretch response of the motor neurons is modelled as an on-off switch with memory, following Boyle, Berri, and Cohen (2012), and the wavelength of the crawl is not written anywhere: it comes out of how slowly a bend develops against the agar. The quick foraging flicks of the nose tip and the pace of the swim rhythm in thin fluid are set by hand to match the measured behaviour rather than emerging from the wiring, and both are tagged as assumptions in the code. Muscle chemistry, egg laying, feeding, and the pharynx are absent, and the dish is two dimensional. The worm on your screen is a hypothesis about how this nervous system produces behaviour, not a copy of the animal; where it behaves like the real one, that is the wiring doing the work.
