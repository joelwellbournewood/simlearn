What am I looking at?

Two rigid arms, joined by a free hinge, swinging under gravity with no friction and no driving force. Four numbers describe the whole system: the two angles, and the two rates at which those angles are changing. It is about as simple as a mechanical system can be while still being unpredictable.

There are many pendulums on screen rather than one. They were released together, and they differ only by the amount in starting spread, which is a fraction of a degree. Every copy obeys the same equations to the same precision, and nothing random is added at any point.

  • For the first few swings they move as one line, because a tiny difference is still tiny.
  • Then the line frays. Small differences are amplified at every swing, roughly multiplying rather than adding, so the gap grows geometrically.
  • Within about ten seconds the copies fill the whole reachable area, and knowing where one of them is tells you nothing about where the others are.

This is sensitive dependence on initial conditions, and it is the point of the demonstration. The system is fully deterministic, so the same starting numbers always give the same motion. It is unpredictable in practice all the same, because to predict twice as far ahead you need roughly the square of the precision in your starting measurement, and no measurement is ever exact.

The same arrangement of two hinged arms turns up in a surprising number of real machines. A crane hook on a slack cable, a wrecking ball, the human leg during a stride, and a golf club through the downswing are all close relatives, which is why crane operators are trained to move the trolley in a particular rhythm and why a bad swing scatters shots so widely. Once two hinged pieces can exchange energy freely, control gets difficult fast.

Release angle is the important slider, because it sets the energy. Start the arms low and the motion stays regular and repetitive, and the copies stay together for a long time. Somewhere above the height at which the lower arm can just swing over the top, the behaviour changes character and the flipping begins. Lower arm length changes the geometry, copies released and trail length change what is drawn, and time speed changes only the playback rate.

Things to try

  • Set spread to its smallest value, a ten-thousandth of a degree, and time how long the fan holds together. Then set it a hundred times wider and time it again. The difference is much smaller than most people expect.
  • Watch the spread now readout climb. Its growth is close to a straight line on a log scale, which is what an exponent looks like.
  • Give the lower arm a length of 1.6 and release from 180 degrees. The tip trace fills the whole disc it can reach.

The demonstration

Press Fan of a hundred. A hundred pendulums are released across a total spread of a tenth of a degree. For the first few seconds they move as one thick line, because for practical purposes they are the same pendulum. Then the line frays and splits, and within about fifteen seconds the hundred copies are scattered over every angle, with no memory of their shared start.

Nothing was added. No noise was injected and no forcing applied. The tiny difference they began with was simply stretched, at a roughly constant exponential rate, until it reached the size of the whole system. That rate is the Lyapunov exponent, and it is the reason weather forecasts stop being useful after about ten days however good the measurements get.

The cost of sensitive dependence

If errors grow exponentially, one more decimal place of accuracy in the starting measurement buys a fixed extra amount of forecast time rather than a proportional one. Instruments ten times better do not give ten times the warning. The horizon belongs to the system itself, and better technology moves it only by a constant.

Regular motion is available too

Drop Release angle to 15 degrees and release the fan again. The copies stay together for minutes. At small amplitudes the system behaves like two coupled linear oscillators and is perfectly well behaved. What decides the character of the motion here is the energy you release it with, and the machine itself is the same either way.

How this one is computed

  • Integration is fourth-order Runge-Kutta with small substeps, and total energy is held to a fraction of a percent over a run. The separation you see on screen is therefore dynamics, and the arithmetic is not contributing to it in any visible way.
  • Given long enough, floating-point error alone would separate two identical copies. That is why the copies here are given a real, stated starting spread instead of being launched identically.
  • The rods are massless and the bobs are points. Real hardware has friction, and eventually stops.

Equations of motion

The standard derivation is Lagrangian. With arm lengths L1, L2, masses m1, m2, and angles θ1, θ2 measured from vertical, the Lagrangian L = T − V is written out and the two Euler-Lagrange equations give a pair of coupled second-order equations that can be arranged as

M(θ1, θ2) · (θ̈1, θ̈2)ᵀ = f(θ1, θ2, θ̇1, θ̇2)

where M is a 2x2 mass matrix depending on the difference of the angles, so the angular accelerations are obtained by inverting a matrix at every timestep. The nonlinearity comes from the sines and cosines of the angles, and from the centrifugal terms in f, which are quadratic in the velocities. There is no closed-form solution and none is expected.

Two degrees of freedom means a four-dimensional phase space. Energy is conserved, which confines the motion to a three-dimensional surface inside it. There are no other conserved quantities, and that absence is what makes chaos possible rather than the number of moving parts: with one more conserved quantity the system would be integrable and every trajectory would be quasi-periodic.

Regular and chaotic motion in the same machine

At low energy the motion is close to two coupled harmonic oscillators. Trajectories lie on invariant tori in phase space, and a Poincare section through them shows closed curves. The Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser theorem guarantees that most of these tori survive a small nonlinear perturbation, which is why gentle swinging is predictable. As energy rises the resonances between the two normal modes overlap, tori break up, and a connected chaotic sea appears in the section, with islands of regular motion embedded in it. The double pendulum is the standard teaching example of this transition because the threshold has a physical meaning you can watch for: chaos becomes widespread once there is enough energy for the lower arm to pass over its own pivot.

Quantifying the divergence

Nearby trajectories separate on average as

δ(t) ≈ δ(0) eλt

where λ is the largest Lyapunov exponent, positive in the chaotic regime and zero on a torus. Rearranged, the horizon of prediction is t ≈ (1/λ) ln(δtol/δ(0)). Because the initial error appears inside a logarithm, improving your measurement by a factor of ten buys the same fixed extra amount of prediction time each time you do it. That logarithm is the whole quantitative content of the phrase butterfly effect, and it applies with equal force to weather models. The fan of copies on screen is a visual estimate of λ, since the time the fan takes to spread to a visible angle scales with 1/λ.

The behaviour survives contact with real hardware. Shinbrot et al. (1992) built a physical double pendulum, measured its divergence and its Poincare sections, and used it as a laboratory demonstration of sensitive dependence, with Levien and Tan (1993) following up on the experimental side.

Numerics, and how to tell physics from arithmetic

The equations here are integrated with fourth-order Runge-Kutta, with each animation frame subdivided into several substeps so the step size stays small when the arms are moving fast. Total energy is displayed live, and its drift is the diagnostic that matters: a symplectic or sufficiently small-step integrator holds it nearly constant, and any visible drift would mean the separation on screen came partly from the integrator's error. In a chaotic system, after a few seconds a single simulated pendulum has departed from the ideal one at any tolerance you care to set, because floating-point rounding is itself a perturbation and then grows as eλt. Statistical properties survive that, meaning how quickly the ensemble spreads and which regions it visits, while the exact position of copy number 37 at t = 20 s is fiction.

Limits of the model

The bobs here are point masses on massless rigid arms, the hinges are frictionless, there is no air resistance, and the motion is confined to a plane. A real double pendulum loses energy and eventually hangs still, so chaos in hardware is a transient, albeit a long one. Adding damping and a periodic drive gives the forced damped pendulum, in which chaos is sustained indefinitely on a strange attractor instead of filling an energy shell, and that version is the closer model for machinery that keeps being fed energy, such as a crane under an operator's hand.
