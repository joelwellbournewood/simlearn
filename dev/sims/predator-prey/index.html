What am I looking at?

Every dot on the field is a single animal with its own energy budget. Hares graze the green patches. Lynx hunt hares. Nobody has written a rule saying the two populations should rise and fall together, and yet the two curves in the graph chase each other for as long as you let it run. The cycle emerges from the animals, and no part of the code contains it.

Each animal repeats the same short list of decisions, every step of its life.

  • Look. It senses only inside its own radius: eyesight for a hare finding grass, hunting range for a lynx finding a hare. Finding nothing, it wanders.
  • Eat. A hare that reaches a green patch gains the energy from grass in it, and the patch is grazed bare until it regrows. A lynx that catches a hare gains the energy from a kill.
  • Spend. Being alive costs the metabolic cost every step. An animal whose energy reaches zero dies.
  • Breed. An animal holding energy above a threshold spends half of it on one offspring, at the birth rate you set.

That is enough to produce the cycle. Plenty of grass means fat hares, and fat hares breed, so the hare curve climbs. A field full of hares is easy hunting, so lynx start to eat well and breed too, but they do it later, because they had to wait for the hares to arrive first. Once there are many lynx the hares are eaten faster than they can breed and the hare curve falls, and the lynx, with nothing left to catch, starve after them. Grass regrows on the empty field and it all begins again. The delay between each cause and its effect is what turns a simple negative feedback into an oscillation.

That lag is worth carrying around, because it explains a great deal outside this field. Fisheries collapse and recover on the same shape of curve, and quotas set from last season's catch arrive a year too late to help. Beef and pork prices have swung on a multi-year cycle since the nineteenth century, for the reason that a farmer's decision to breed pays off long after the price that prompted it. Anything that responds to a signal it can only measure in arrears tends to overshoot in both directions.

The sliders fall into three groups. Energy from grass, energy from a kill, and plant regrowth decide how much energy enters the system, which sets the ceiling on how many animals it can carry. Metabolic cost and birth rate decide how fast that energy is turned into animals and lost again, which sets how violent the swings are. Eyesight, hunting range, and territoriality decide how easily the two species find each other, and how much the lynx get in each other's way. Rich grass with sharp-eyed lynx gives tall, spiky booms that often end with one side extinct, and a lean field gives a flatter, duller, and much more durable world.

Things to try

  • Switch the trace to Phase orbit and watch the loop close on itself.
  • Run Paradox of enrichment and watch the swings widen until one of them hits the floor.
  • Raise Hunting range in the middle of a run and see how few steps it takes to empty the field.
  • Set Plant regrowth to 1 and count how few animals the land can carry.
  • Remove the lynx entirely with Starting lynx at 0, then watch the hares stop at the grass ceiling instead of growing without limit.
  • Take Territoriality from 0 to 10. At 0 the lynx pile onto the same hares and travel as a pack. Pushed up, they hold each other at arm's length and cover the field evenly, and above about 4 they usually starve within a minute: keeping your distance costs a predator kills.

Why the lynx peak arrives late

Each population is driven by the size of the other one, and that delay is why the lynx peak always arrives after the hare peak rather than with it, by roughly a quarter of a cycle. Alfred Lotka and Vito Volterra described the same lag with a pair of differential equations in the 1920s. Switch the trace to Phase orbit to watch it directly: the graph drops time and plots hares against lynx, so the state of the whole field becomes one point tracing a loop.

Lynx keep away from other lynx

Canada lynx are solitary. Adults hold home ranges, hunt alone, and tolerate each other mainly at the edges, so a model in which every lynx simply runs at the nearest hare gets one thing badly wrong: the predators collapse into a travelling pack, because they all pick the same target. Territoriality gives each lynx a personal space, measured here from a body length at 1 up to about a fifth of the field at 10. A rival inside it pushes the animal away, hard while it is patrolling and only slightly while it is actually running down a hare, since a hungry cat does not break off a chase because it can see a neighbour.

The consequence is worth watching, because it is not the one most people expect. Spacing out covers the ground more evenly, which sounds like better hunting, but each animal also spends part of its budget travelling away from food rather than towards it. Measured here over a minute of simulated time on the default settings, the lynx survive every run with territoriality at 1 and starve in most runs at 4 or above. Ecologists call that interference competition, as distinct from the competition for the prey itself, and it is one of the reasons solitary carnivores end up with ranges far larger than the food in them would require.

Energy sets the ceiling

Follow the energy and the numbers stop being mysterious. Sunlight is stored in grass, some of that reaches a hare, and a smaller share again reaches a lynx, because every animal burns energy simply staying alive. That loss at each step is why any healthy field holds far more hares than lynx. It is also why food chains are short, why top predators are rare enough to be the first thing a shrinking habitat loses, and why a given area of land feeds many more people growing crops than grazing cattle.

The Plant regrowth slider is the carrying capacity of the land, the ceiling on how many hares it can feed. Turn it down, as the Sparse habitat preset does, and you get a small population living close to the edge. Turning it up is riskier than it sounds. Rich land lets the hares boom higher, which lets the lynx boom higher, and the swings grow until one of the crashes reaches zero. Michael Rosenzweig named this the paradox of enrichment in 1971, and the preset of that name sets it up for you. The same warning applies to nutrient runoff into a lake, where fertilising the bottom of a food web has repeatedly destabilised everything above it.

The textbook model, and how this one differs

The classical description is the Lotka-Volterra system, derived independently by Lotka (1925) and Volterra (1926), with prey N and predators P:

dN/dt = rN − aNP
dP/dt = εaNP − mP

Prey grow exponentially, predators meet prey by mass action at rate aNP, convert them to offspring with efficiency ε, and die at rate m. The system has a neutrally stable centre, so it produces closed cycles whose amplitude is fixed entirely by the initial conditions, with a period of about 2π/√(rm) near the equilibrium, and predators peaking a quarter of a cycle behind prey. That quarter-period lag is the one prediction from the model that survives essentially every later modification. The neutral stability is an artefact, and any small change to the functional form turns the cycles into either a spiral into equilibrium or a growing oscillation.

The Rosenzweig-MacArthur model repairs the two worst assumptions by giving prey logistic self-limitation and predators a saturating Holling type II functional response, a/(1 + ahN). This has a stable equilibrium that loses stability through a Hopf bifurcation as prey carrying capacity rises, producing a genuine limit cycle whose amplitude grows until the trough of the cycle is low enough for extinction. That is the paradox of enrichment in its formal dress: adding resources to a predator and prey system can destabilise it and drive both species extinct. The energy from grass and plant regrowth sliders in this simulation are that experiment, and they reproduce the effect.

What individual-based means here

Nothing in this code integrates those equations, and there is no aNP term anywhere in it. Encounters happen because one animal is inside another's search radius, so the effective attack rate emerges from movement speed, sensing radius, and the spatial arrangement of both populations. Three things follow that the differential equations cannot express. Populations are integers, so a trough of two animals behaves quite differently from a trough of 0.4: the classical equations let a population fall to a fraction of an animal and recover, while here the last few animals can simply fail to meet and the species is gone for good. That is demographic stochasticity, and it is a large part of why conservation biologists talk about a minimum viable population rather than about keeping the number above zero. Animals are individuals with unequal energy, so selection and variance in reproductive success exist. And the field has space, so populations decorrelate locally: prey survive in patches the predators have not reached, and you can watch waves travel across the arena while the population as a whole does something else. Spatial refuges of exactly this kind were what allowed Huffaker (1958) to keep a predatory mite and its prey coexisting in the laboratory, where a simple homogeneous arena, as in Gause's (1934) experiments, gave rapid extinction. The practical descendant of that result is the refuge strategy in pest control, where a deliberately untreated patch of crop is left standing to stop the pest population from being pushed through the bottleneck that selects for resistance.

The hare and lynx record, and what it can support

The hare and lynx pelt records of the Hudson's Bay Company, popularised by Elton and Nicholson (1942), show a cycle of roughly ten years over more than a century, and they appear in every textbook next to these equations. The modern interpretation is more complicated than predation alone. The Kluane project, a decade of large-scale field manipulations summarised by Krebs et al. (1995), found that excluding predators roughly doubled hare density, adding food roughly tripled it, and doing both together increased it far more than the sum of the two, so food supply and predation interact rather than acting independently. Pelt records also measure trapping effort alongside animal abundance. The cycle you see here is genuine behaviour of this model, and it should be read as an illustration of the mechanism rather than as evidence about Canadian lynx.

Limits of the model

The world here contains one prey species, one predator species, and one resource. Animals have no age, no territory, no disease, and no capacity to evolve the parameters you set, and energy serves as a single currency for movement, survival, and reproduction alike. The arena is also closed, so a population that reaches zero can never recover, whereas real local extinctions are usually refilled from neighbouring populations, which is the whole argument for wildlife corridors. Treat this as a demonstration of where cycles and extinctions come from, and of what changes once averaged rates are replaced by countable individuals.
