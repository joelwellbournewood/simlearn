What am I looking at?

A patch of nerve membrane, magnified, running the equations Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley fitted to the giant axon of the squid in 1952. The picture on the left is the membrane itself, with the channels sitting in it and the ions on either side of it. The trace on the right is the voltage across that membrane, measured in millivolts, as it changes.

Three things set that voltage, and only three.

  • The gradients. There is more sodium outside the cell than inside, and more potassium inside than outside. Each ion therefore has a voltage it would drag the membrane to if it were the only one that could cross: about +50 mV for sodium, about −77 mV for potassium.
  • The gates. Sodium and potassium cross through separate channels, and those channels open and close depending on the voltage they currently sit at. Sodium opens fast, then closes itself off. Potassium opens slowly and stays open.
  • The pump. The Na/K pump spends energy putting the ions back where they started, which is what keeps the gradients from running down.

Everything you see follows from those. At rest, potassium leaks out and holds the membrane near −65 mV. Push the voltage up far enough with the electrode and sodium channels open, sodium floods in, and the voltage those channels are responding to is the same voltage they are changing. The rise feeds itself, and the spike goes all the way to the top with no further help from you. Sodium then shuts itself off while the slow potassium channels are still opening, so the voltage overshoots downward before settling. The whole event takes about two milliseconds.

That two milliseconds is the unit every nervous system is built from. Every thought, every sensation, and every muscle you move is carried by this event repeated along billions of fibres, which is why so much of medicine ends up interfering with it deliberately. The injection at the dentist works by blocking the sodium channel in this model. Pufferfish poisoning blocks the same channel with a toxin, and the patient stays awake and paralysed. Several drugs for epilepsy, for heart rhythm, and for chronic pain act on close relatives of these gates, and the reason a nerve conduction test in a clinic can locate an injury is that the speed of this spike along a nerve is measurable to the metre per second.

Fire pulse injects current at the strength and duration set in the panel. The interesting settings are the ones near the edge: just under threshold gives nothing at all, just over gives a full-height spike, and a second pulse arriving too early gives nothing however hard you push, because the sodium channels have not reset yet. The electrode chips change what the apparatus is doing rather than what the axon is doing. Current clamp injects current and lets the voltage move, while voltage clamp holds the voltage where you put it and measures the current needed, which is the experiment the equations were fitted to. The drug chips block one channel type each, and each chip explains itself if you hover it.

The three gates

Hodgkin and Huxley described the whole thing with three numbers between 0 and 1, plotted under the voltage trace:

  • m, the sodium activation gate. Fast, and it opens on depolarisation. Sodium conductance goes as m³, so three of these have to line up.
  • h, the sodium inactivation gate. Slow, and it works backwards, since depolarisation shuts it. This is what ends the spike, and it is why you cannot fire again immediately.
  • n, the potassium gate. Slow, opens on depolarisation, and its conductance goes as n⁴. Potassium leaving pulls the voltage back down and overshoots past rest.

A sodium channel conducts only when all three m subunits and the h gate are open at once. In the theatre the individual channels flicker independently, sampled from the same rate constants that drive the smooth curves below. Real channels are stochastic, and the smooth curve is what you get by averaging thousands of them.

C dV/dt = Istim − ḡNa·m³h·(V−50) − ḡK·n⁴·(V+77) − 0.3·(V+54.4)

Why it is all-or-none

Below threshold the leak wins and the voltage sags back. Above threshold the sodium feedback wins and the spike goes to the same height however hard you pushed. Nudge the pulse strength half a unit at a time through 7 µA/cm² and watch the trace snap from one behaviour to the other. There is no half-spike. This is also why a nerve carries intensity as a firing rate: a single fibre cannot make a bigger spike to signal a harder pinch, so it makes more of them.

Why you have to wait

Straight after a spike, h is near zero, which means the sodium channels are inactivated, plugged from the inside by their own ball-and-chain, and n is still high, so potassium is still pouring out. Set the sharp electrode to two pulses and shorten the gap. At 16 milliseconds the second spike fires normally. Below about 8 milliseconds nothing works at any strength, which is the absolute refractory period, and it is why a spike only ever travels forwards down an axon. In between, the second spike is possible and costs more current.

Four ways to put an electrode on it

The recording configuration decides what the traces are able to show you, because each one is a physically different experiment.

  • Sharp microelectrode. A fine tip through the membrane, running freely. You set the current with a second electrode and the membrane picks its own voltage. This is the only way to watch an action potential happen by itself.
  • Whole-cell current clamp. A blunt pipette sealed on and the patch broken through, passing a set current for a set time, one sweep at a time. Raise the command a step at a time and the sweeps stack up behind each other: nothing below about 2.5 µA/cm², then one spike, then a train, then the membrane jams.
  • Whole-cell voltage clamp. The amplifier holds the voltage wherever you put it and reports the current it has to supply to keep it there. This is the experiment the equations came from. Step to a few different voltages and the family builds up: a fast inward sodium current that grows, peaks around 0 mV, and vanishes at +50 mV, which is ENa, and a slow outward potassium current that just keeps growing. Add TEA or TTX to take one of them away.
  • Cell-attached patch. The seal is left intact, so you record only the three sodium channels under the tip. Openings are square steps of about one picoamp, one protein at a time. Most sweeps show nothing at all, which is exactly why you have to average. Leave it repeating and the average of the flickering turns into the smooth macroscopic current from the voltage-clamp trace.

On the whole axon the choice is between microelectrodes pushed inside, which give clean monophasic spikes, and electrodes resting on the surface, which is what a clinical nerve conduction study actually records: much smaller, biphasic, and with the same delay between the two sites.

The drugs

Five things to put in the bath. Blocked channels go grey and show the drug molecule lodged in the pore, and the partial blockers take out a fraction of the channels, which is what a partial block physically is.

  • TTX plugs the sodium pore from outside, completely. The membrane can still be charged by the electrode, and it cannot make a spike of its own.
  • Lidocaine takes out about 40% of the sodium channels from inside. That alone raises threshold roughly eightfold. Push the pulse to 100 µA/cm² and a normal spike comes back, which is the difference between an anaesthetic and a poison.
  • TEA blocks the delayed rectifier from inside. The background potassium channels that hold the resting potential are a different, TEA-insensitive population and stay open, so the membrane still sits at −65 mV. The spike fires taller and repolarisation is left to the slow leak.
  • 4-AP is a milder potassium block. Try it on a stripped patch in the axon view.
  • Pronase digests the inactivation gate off most sodium channels. Nothing shuts the sodium current off, so the spike becomes a permanent plateau near +20 mV. Wash it out to recover.

The whole axon

Switch to Whole axon and the same equations run at two hundred points along a cable, coupled by the resistance of the cytoplasm. A spike at one end charges its neighbour, which spikes and charges the next. Two recording electrodes sit 4.2 cm apart, and that distance divided by the delay between their traces is the conduction velocity.

Bare, the model axon runs at roughly 12 m/s. Wrap it in myelin and the internode barely stores charge, so current from one node reaches the next almost undiminished and the spike jumps node to node three times faster. Strip the sheath off a patch and two things go wrong: the bare stretch soaks up charge, and potassium channels that were sequestered under the wrap are now exposed and shunt the signal away. Past a few millimetres, conduction stops dead. Now add 4-AP or TEA. The exposed potassium channels are blocked and conduction comes back, and that is the logic behind giving 4-aminopyridine to some people with multiple sclerosis.

The equations

The membrane is treated as a capacitor in parallel with three conductances, giving one current-balance equation and three gating equations:

Cm dV/dt = I − ḡNa m³h (V − ENa) − ḡK n⁴ (V − EK) − gL (V − EL)
dx/dt = αx(V)(1 − x) − βx(V) x  for x = m, h, n

m is sodium activation, h is sodium inactivation, and n is potassium activation. Each is a probability between 0 and 1, relaxing toward a voltage-dependent steady state with a voltage-dependent time constant. The exponents carry real content. Hodgkin and Huxley chose m³h and n⁴ because those powers fitted the sigmoid onset of the measured conductances, and the numbers were later read as the count of independent gating particles that must be in place for a channel to conduct. In the original paper the maximal conductances are 120 mS/cm² for sodium and 36 mS/cm² for potassium, with a leak of 0.3, at 6.3 °C. Rate constants scale steeply with temperature, roughly a factor of 3 per 10 °C, which is why a squid axon at room temperature fires a much narrower spike.

Where the numbers came from

The measurement problem was that membrane current depends on voltage while voltage depends on current. Cole and Marmont's feedback amplifier, the voltage clamp, broke the loop by holding the voltage at a commanded value and recording the current the amplifier had to supply. Hodgkin and Huxley combined that with ion substitution to separate the sodium and potassium components, published the analysis as a series of papers in the Journal of Physiology in 1952, and integrated the resulting equations by hand on a mechanical calculator, which took weeks per simulated spike. The predicted action potential shape, threshold, and conduction velocity all matched recordings that had not been used in the fit. They shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with John Eccles. The voltage clamp mode in this simulation is that experiment, and the current traces it produces are the ones the model was built from.

Threshold emerges from the dynamics

There is no comparator in these equations and no value written down as a firing level. The apparent threshold is where the inward sodium current begins to exceed the outward potassium and leak currents, so that the positive feedback loop wins. In the space-clamped model it is a very steep boundary rather than a mathematically sharp one, and in dynamical terms it corresponds to the boundary between the basins of attraction around the resting state, with the transition to repetitive firing under steady current occurring through a Hopf bifurcation. Izhikevich (2007) sets out the classification. This is why threshold in this simulation is measured by running the model instead of being looked up, since adding a channel blocker to the bath moves it a long way.

Refractoriness, blockers, and propagation

The absolute refractory period is h, sodium inactivation, sitting near zero, and no stimulus of any size can recruit channels that are inactivated. The relative refractory period that follows is h recovering while n is still elevated, so a larger stimulus is required and the resulting spike is smaller. Tetrodotoxin, the pufferfish toxin, binds the outer pore of the voltage-gated sodium channel and abolishes the spike while leaving the potassium current intact, a selectivity that made it a standard tool for dissecting the two currents (Narahashi, Moore, & Scott, 1964). Tetraethylammonium does the reverse and blocks the potassium channel, broadening the spike.

Propagation along an axon needs one more ingredient, the cable equation, in which local circuit current from an active patch depolarises the patch ahead of it. In an unmyelinated fibre conduction velocity scales with the square root of diameter, which is the reason the squid evolved a millimetre-wide axon to work its escape jet and the reason Hodgkin and Huxley could get an electrode inside one. Myelin raises membrane resistance and lowers capacitance between nodes, so the depolarisation jumps from node to node, which Huxley and Stampfli (1949) demonstrated in single frog fibres. The myelinated axon in this simulation is a chain of Hodgkin-Huxley nodes coupled by axial resistance, and stripping the sheath slows or blocks it in the way a demyelinating lesion does.

Limits of the model

The concentration gradients are held fixed here. A real spike moves about one sodium ion in a hundred thousand and the pump carries it back out over the following seconds, so nothing in this model runs the gradients down. Blocking the pump in a real preparation shrinks the spike over minutes of firing, and that is an experiment this simulation does not offer. The pump drawn on screen runs far too fast, and the drawn channels are far too few and far too large, since a square micrometre of real axon holds hundreds of them. The myelinated axon is a lumped node-and-internode cable rather than an anatomically faithful one, so its velocities have the right shape and only roughly the right size.

The equations themselves are deterministic, while real channels are stochastic, so this model has no channel noise and no spontaneous firing. These are also the 1952 squid equations at 6.3 °C, held there throughout, and a mammalian axon at body temperature uses faster kinetics and additional channel types. Real neurons carry a dozen or more channel types, including calcium and several potassium variants that the squid axon largely lacks, and the shapes of spikes in mammalian cortex depend on them. Later structural work also showed that inactivation is a separate mechanism from activation rather than a fourth identical gating particle. Within the range they were fitted to, the equations remain accurate, and they are the foundation of every conductance-based neuron model since.
