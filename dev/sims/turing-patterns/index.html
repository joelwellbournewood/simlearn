What am I looking at?

A square of tissue with two chemicals dissolved in it, and no plan anywhere for what it should look like. Every cell starts identical to every other one. Nothing in the code contains the pattern you end up with. It appears because a perfectly even mixture turns out to be an unstable thing to be.

Two substances react and spread, and that is the whole mechanism.

  • The activator makes more of itself, consuming the second chemical as it goes. Wherever there is slightly more of it than average, it builds up faster still.
  • The substrate is fed in evenly from outside at the feed rate F, and it spreads through the sheet about twice as fast as the activator does.
  • Everything is removed at the kill rate k, so a blob that stops being fed dies back.

The competition between those two is what makes shapes. A patch of activator grows, and while it grows it starves the ring of tissue around it, because the substrate it needs is drained from a wider area than the patch itself covers. That ring cannot start a patch of its own, so the next patch has to begin further out, at a distance set by how far the two chemicals spread. Local growth with longer-range suppression gives you a spacing, and a spacing is a pattern.

Once you have seen it, the same logic shows up in odd places. Vegetation in semi-arid country grows in regular stripes and rings, because each plant draws water from the bare ground around it. Ripples in sand, the spacing of hair follicles, the ridges on the roof of your mouth, and the spots on a big cat are all thought to come from something with this shape to it. Alan Turing proposed the idea in 1952, in the last paper he published, and it took forty years and a good deal of argument before biologists were persuaded.

Only three sliders change the chemistry: feed rate, kill rate, and the diffusion ratio between the two substances. The map beside the field shows where in that space you are, and the regions on it are what the presets pick out. Small moves matter, since the boundary between spots, stripes, and a labyrinth is a few thousandths of a unit wide, which is why nudging one slider can turn a settled pattern into a growing one. Everything else in the panel either paints on the field or changes how it is drawn, apart from growth and anisotropy, which stretch the tissue while the pattern forms and are how a real pattern gets aligned with the body it sits on.

Turing's idea

In 1952 Alan Turing pointed out something counterintuitive: diffusion, which normally smooths differences out, can create them. If a self-amplifying chemical is held in check by a second one that spreads faster, a uniform mixture becomes unstable. Any tiny fluctuation grows locally while suppressing its neighbourhood, and the system settles into a pattern with a characteristic spacing. Turing called the chemicals morphogens and suggested they were how embryos decide where to put things.

This particular pair is the Gray-Scott model, an autocatalytic reaction in a stirred tank rewritten as a field. u is the substrate, v the activator, and the reaction is u + 2v → 3v.

∂u/∂t = Du∇²u − uv² + F(1 − u) ∂v/∂t = Dv∇²v + uv² − (F + k)v

F is the feed rate, the rate at which substrate is topped up towards 1. k is the extra kill rate on the activator. Those two numbers are what the map navigates, and most presets change nothing else. Three of them go further. Labyrinth lowers both diffusion constants together, which shrinks the spacing without moving on the map. Stripes switches on the fast axis, since an isotropic field gives a maze and never parallel stripes. Worms runs more steps per frame, because its filaments crawl slowly.

Each preset is a complete recipe. Clicking one sets the feed and kill rates, the diffusion ratio, the fast axis, the seed, the speed, and the colours, and puts everything it does not name back to the default, so a preset always produces the pattern it is named after. Move any control that changes the chemistry and the selection drops to Free play, which is the card to start from when you want to mix your own.

Why the map has that shape

The two curves on the parameter map are solved directly from the equations with diffusion switched off, so they mark real boundaries in the chemistry rather than drawn guides.

  • The upper arc is where the non-trivial steady states collide and vanish. Above it, the only uniform state is the dead one, u = 1 and v = 0. Patterns still live up there, and only as isolated structures that must keep dividing to survive, which is why the mitosis preset sits just above the line.
  • The lower curve is a Hopf bifurcation. To its left the steady state loses stability by oscillating in time rather than by splitting in space, so what you get is travelling waves and chemical turbulence. The waves and chaos presets sit right on it.
  • The olive patch is the only region where a classical Turing instability actually applies: a uniform steady state that is stable on its own and goes unstable once diffusion is switched on. This one depends on how much faster the substrate spreads, unlike the two curves. At the default ratio of 2 it is a thin sliver on the right, and dragging the diffusion ratio up opens it out across the map.

Every preset in the list sits within a few thousandths of one of the two curves. The whole zoo lives in a sliver, which is why the map is worth navigating by clicking rather than by typing numbers.

The growing field

A real embryo changes size while it is being patterned. A somite-stage vertebrate trunk, a fish flank, and a limb bud are all getting bigger as the pattern forms. When you switch on growth here, the tissue stretches lengthwise while the chemistry holds on to its own preferred spacing. The stripes therefore refuse to stretch with the animal, and new ones insert between the old ones. That is exactly what Kondo and Asai observed in the marine angelfish Pomacanthus in 1995: as the fish grows, its stripes keep a constant width and new stripes appear in between, while a painted-on pattern would simply have scaled up.

The field is drawn in material coordinates, so what you see is the tissue itself elongating. Watch the stripe count in the readout climb while the visible spacing stays put.

Directional diffusion

Diffusion in tissue is often faster along one axis than another, because cells are elongated, or aligned, or the extracellular matrix is fibrous. Turning on directional diffusion replaces the single diffusion constant with a tensor. Structures then line up, since the pattern's wavelength is longer along the fast axis, so stripes tend to run across it. This is the difference between the Stripes preset and the Labyrinth one, and it changes the physics rather than the appearance. With diffusion equal in every direction the model has no reason to prefer any direction, so it always produces a maze, and parallel stripes require either an anisotropic tissue or a boundary telling the pattern which way to line up. The strength is capped at 3× because beyond that the explicit solver used here stops being stable.

Are these actually Turing patterns?

Partly, and it is worth being precise about it. Turing's own argument is a linear one: take a uniform steady state that is stable on its own, add diffusion, and show that a perturbation now grows. That condition is the olive patch on the map, and at the standard diffusion ratio of 2 not one of the presets sits inside it. Most of them sit where no non-trivial uniform state exists at all, or where the state oscillates. In those cases what you are watching is a self-sustaining localized structure, a pulse of activator that eats outward, splits when it gets too broad, and starves where the substrate has run out.

That gap is why Pearson's 1993 paper on this model is called Complex patterns in a simple system. The ingredients are Turing's and so is the characteristic spacing, and the linear analysis Turing wrote down still does not by itself explain a spot that divides. Push the diffusion ratio above about 3 and the olive region swells to cover the right-hand presets, which is the regime where the textbook version does hold.

Under the hood

What runs here is chemistry on a grid, built from four choices that keep a very stiff system stable at interactive speed.

  • Explicit forward Euler on a periodic grid, so the left edge wraps to the right and the top to the bottom.
  • A nine-point diffusion stencil, generalised to a diffusion tensor so that the directional and growth modes stay stable.
  • Growth is handled in material coordinates: the diffusion term along the growing axis is divided by the square of the stretch factor, and both chemicals are diluted at the rate the tissue expands.
  • The whole thing runs on the GPU in a pair of floating-point textures.

The equations on screen

This is the Gray-Scott model, a two-species reaction-diffusion system for concentrations u, the substrate, and v, the activator, on a grid with periodic boundaries:

∂u/∂t = Du ∇²u − u v² + F(1 − u)
∂v/∂t = Dv ∇²v + u v² − (F + k) v

The cubic term u v² is the autocatalytic step, in which two molecules of v convert one of u into a third v. F is the rate at which fresh u is pumped in and everything is washed out, and k is an extra decay applied to v alone. Pearson (1993) mapped the behaviour of this system across the F-k plane and named the regimes, and the map in the panel is that parameter plane with his classification on it.

Why a uniform state is unstable

Turing (1952) asked what happens to a homogeneous chemical steady state when you perturb it slightly, and answered it by linearising the system and asking which spatial Fourier modes grow. A perturbation of wavenumber q grows or decays according to the eigenvalues of the Jacobian of the reaction terms with the diffusion terms −D q² added along the diagonal. If diffusion is absent, or equal for both species, the steady state is stable. If the inhibitory species diffuses sufficiently faster than the autocatalytic one, a band of intermediate wavenumbers acquires a positive growth rate while q = 0 and large q stay negative. The state is then stable against uniform change and unstable against change at one particular length scale, which is why the resulting pattern has a characteristic spacing rather than a characteristic size. This is a diffusion-driven instability, and it is the sense in which the pattern is created by the very process that usually smooths things out.

Gierer and Meinhardt (1972) restated the requirement in the form that is now standard in developmental biology: short-range activation with long-range inhibition. The diffusion ratio slider is that condition made adjustable. Turn it down toward 1 and every pattern in the system dissolves, however you set F and k. The same two-ingredient recipe has since been used to explain vegetation banding in the Sahel, where plants are the activator and soil water the long-range inhibitor, and the periodicity of those bands is now studied as an early-warning signal for desertification.

How much of this happens in animals

Turing's paper was theory with no molecular candidates, and for forty years the competing account of pattern in embryos was positional information, in which cells read a pre-existing gradient and consult a lookup table (Wolpert, 1969). The evidence for genuine Turing mechanisms came later, and it now covers several systems. Zebrafish pigment stripes arise from short-range and long-range interactions between pigment cell types, in which the diffusing species are cells and signalling ranges rather than freely diffusing molecules, and the mathematics belongs to the same family regardless. Kondo and Asai (1995) found the angelfish stripes described above, which a fixed prepattern cannot produce. Sick et al. (2006) identified WNT and its diffusible inhibitor DKK as an activator-inhibitor pair setting mouse hair follicle spacing. Economou et al. (2012) showed the periodic ridges of the mouse palate form by a Turing mechanism, and Sheth et al. (2012) showed that reducing Hox gene dose increases the number of digits in a way consistent with Turing-type periodic patterning of the limb bud. The modern position, argued in reviews such as Green and Sharpe (2015), is that real embryos use both ideas together: gradients position and orient the field, and local self-organising instabilities fill it in with periodic structure. On the chemistry side, the instability was finally produced in a controlled experiment in 1990, in the chlorite-iodide-malonic acid reaction, nearly four decades after Turing predicted it.

Limits of the model

Gray-Scott is a caricature of a chemistry, and no animal runs this exact reaction. What runs here is a chemistry rather than a tissue. There are no cells, no mechanics, no gene regulation, and no growth beyond the artificial stretch this simulation applies. Two chemicals is the bare minimum for the instability, while real patterning networks have many more components, and that changes which patterns are reachable. Nothing here is fitted to measurements from any organism, so the resemblance to a shell, a coat, or a coral is qualitative. It is evidence about what kind of mechanism could produce such a thing, and any claim that it did produce it has to come from the biology.
