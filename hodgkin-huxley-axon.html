What am I looking at?

A membrane doing the one trick that makes nervous systems possible.

An axon at rest sits at about −65 millivolts, inside negative. That voltage is held up by two opposing gradients: sodium is piled up outside, potassium is piled up inside. In the theatre view each dot is an ion, and the lopsided counts above and below the membrane are the gradients themselves — drawn in the ratios this model's reversal potentials imply, about eight sodium ions outside for every one inside, and about twenty-four potassium inside for every one outside. Chloride and the big organic anions are drawn too, dimmer, because each side has to come out electrically neutral. Nothing moves until a channel opens.

Push a little current in and the voltage creeps up. Push enough and the membrane stops being a passive thing and takes over: sodium channels open, sodium floods in, that drives the voltage up further, which opens more sodium channels. The spike is that runaway, caught and killed about a millisecond later by two slower processes.

The three gates

Hodgkin and Huxley described the whole thing with three numbers between 0 and 1, plotted under the voltage trace:

  • m — the sodium activation gate. Fast. Opens on depolarisation. Sodium conductance goes as m³, so three of these have to line up.
  • h — the sodium inactivation gate. Slow, and it works backwards: depolarisation shuts it. This is what ends the spike, and it is why you cannot fire again immediately.
  • n — the potassium gate. Slow, opens on depolarisation, conductance goes as n⁴. Potassium leaving pulls the voltage back down and overshoots past rest.

A sodium channel conducts only when all three m subunits and the h gate are open at once. In the theatre the individual channels flicker independently, sampled from the same rate constants that drive the smooth curves below — real channels are stochastic, and the smooth curve is what you get by averaging thousands of them.

C dV/dt = Istim − ḡNa·m³h·(V−50) − ḡK·n⁴·(V+77) − 0.3·(V+54.4)

Why it is all-or-none

Below threshold the leak wins and the voltage sags back. Above threshold the sodium feedback wins and the spike goes to the same height no matter how hard you pushed. Nudge the pulse strength half a unit at a time through 7 µA/cm² and watch the trace snap from one behaviour to the other. There is no half-spike.

Why you have to wait

Straight after a spike, h is near zero — the sodium channels are inactivated, plugged from the inside by their own ball-and-chain — and n is still high, so potassium is still pouring out. Set the sharp electrode to two pulses and shorten the gap. At 16 milliseconds the second spike fires normally. Below about 8 milliseconds nothing works at any strength: that is the absolute refractory period, and it is why a spike only ever travels forwards down an axon. In between, the second spike is possible but costs more current.

Four ways to put an electrode on it

The recording configuration is not just a picture: each one changes what the traces can show you, because each is a different experiment.

  • Sharp microelectrode — a fine tip through the membrane, running freely. You set the current with a second electrode and the membrane picks its own voltage. This is the only way to watch an action potential happen by itself.
  • Whole-cell current clamp — a blunt pipette sealed on and the patch broken through, passing a set current for a set time, one sweep at a time. Raise the command a step at a time and the sweeps stack up behind each other: nothing below about 2.5 µA/cm², then one spike, then a train, then the membrane jams.
  • Whole-cell voltage clamp — the amplifier holds the voltage wherever you put it and reports the current it has to supply to keep it there. This is the experiment the equations came from. Step to a few different voltages and the family builds up: a fast inward sodium current that grows, peaks around 0 mV and vanishes at +50 mV, which is ENa, and a slow outward potassium current that just keeps growing. Add TEA or TTX to take one of them away.
  • Cell-attached patch — the seal is left intact, so you record only the three sodium channels under the tip. Openings are square steps of about one picoamp, one protein at a time. Most sweeps show nothing at all, which is exactly why you have to average; leave it repeating and the average of the flickering turns into the smooth macroscopic current from the voltage-clamp trace.

On the whole axon the choice is between microelectrodes pushed inside, which give clean monophasic spikes, and electrodes resting on the surface, which is what a clinical nerve conduction study actually records: much smaller, biphasic, and the same delay between the two sites.

The drugs

Six things to put in the bath. Blocked channels go grey and show the drug molecule lodged in the pore, and the partial blockers take out a fraction of the channels — which is what a partial block physically is.

  • TTX plugs the sodium pore from outside, completely. The membrane can still be charged by the electrode, but it cannot make a spike of its own.
  • Lidocaine takes out about 40% of the sodium channels from inside. That alone raises threshold roughly eightfold. Push the pulse to 100 µA/cm² and a normal spike comes back, which is the difference between an anaesthetic and a poison.
  • TEA blocks the delayed rectifier from inside. The background potassium channels that hold the resting potential are a different, TEA-insensitive population and stay open, so the membrane still sits at −65 mV. The spike fires taller and repolarisation is left to the slow leak.
  • 4-AP is a milder potassium block. Try it on a stripped patch in the axon view.
  • Pronase digests the inactivation gate off most sodium channels. Nothing shuts the sodium current off, so the spike becomes a permanent plateau near +20 mV. Wash it out to recover.
  • Ouabain stops the sodium–potassium pump. Watch sodium pile up inside, spike after spike, with nothing carrying it back out.

The whole axon

Switch to Whole axon and the same equations run at two hundred points along a cable, coupled by the resistance of the cytoplasm. A spike at one end charges its neighbour, which spikes and charges the next. Two recording electrodes sit 4.2 cm apart; that distance divided by the delay between their traces is the conduction velocity.

Bare, the model axon runs at roughly 12 m/s. Wrap it in myelin and the internode barely stores charge, so current from one node reaches the next almost undiminished and the spike jumps node to node three times faster. Strip the sheath off a patch and two things go wrong: the bare stretch soaks up charge, and potassium channels that were sequestered under the wrap are now exposed and shunt the signal away. Past a few millimetres, conduction stops dead. Then add 4-AP or TEA — the exposed potassium channels are blocked and conduction comes back. That is the logic behind giving 4-aminopyridine to some people with multiple sclerosis.

What this model does not do

The concentration gradients are treated as fixed; a real spike moves a vanishing fraction of the ions and the pump handles the rest over seconds. The pump drawn here runs far too fast, and the drawn channels are far too few and far too big — a square micrometre of real axon holds hundreds. The myelinated axon is a lumped node-and-internode cable rather than an anatomically faithful one, so its velocities are the right shape but only roughly the right size. And these are the 1952 squid equations at 6.3 °C, held there throughout: mammalian axons at body temperature use faster kinetics and additional channel types.
