What am I looking at?

A collection of bodies, each pulling on every other with Newton's law of gravitation: the force grows with both masses and falls off with the square of the distance. That is the entire rule. There is no code for "orbit", no code for "galaxy", no code for "Lagrange point". All of those follow from the one rule.

Kepler's laws, for free

Click any body. The dashed curve is the orbit it would follow if only its main attractor existed: an ellipse if it is bound, a hyperbola if it is not. The card shows the eccentricity, the semi-major axis and the period. Watch the comet in Star and planets: it crawls at the far end and whips around the near end, sweeping equal areas in equal times. Compare two planets and you will find the period grows as the semi-major axis to the power 1.5. Nobody told the simulation any of that.

Why two bodies are easy and three are not

Two bodies have an exact solution, written down in 1687. Add a third and no such formula exists. Poincare proved in the 1890s that the general three-body problem is chaotic: two starts a hair apart end up somewhere completely different. Pythagorean three is the classic test case, posed by Burrau in 1913 and only solved numerically by Szebehely and Peters in 1967. Three bodies with masses 3, 4 and 5 start at rest on the corners of a 3-4-5 triangle. After a series of close passes one is flung away and the other two settle into a binary. That is the usual ending for three bodies: chaos resolves itself by throwing one out.

The figure-eight

Figure-eight is one of the few known periodic solutions for three equal masses. Moore found it numerically in 1993 and Chenciner and Montgomery proved it exists in 2000. Each body follows the same lemniscate, a third of a period behind the one in front. It is stable to small nudges, which is why it survives here, but no real system is known to do it. Try flinging a tiny body through the middle and see how long the choreography holds.

Lagrange points

Around a star and planet in circular orbit there are five places where a small body can sit still relative to the pair. Euler found the three on the line through them in the 1760s; Lagrange found the two at the corners of equilateral triangles in 1772. Switch to the co-rotating view and they stop moving. L4 and L5 are stable if the planet is under about 1/25 the mass of the star: Jupiter's Trojan asteroids have sat there for billions of years. L1, L2 and L3 are saddle points; anything placed there drifts off within a few orbits, which is why the James Webb telescope at Sun-Earth L2 fires thrusters every few weeks. The bodies on nearly the same orbit as the planet trace horseshoes in the rotating frame. Saturn's moons Janus and Epimetheus do exactly this, swapping orbits every four years.

The slingshot

Send a light body past a heavy moving one and it can leave much faster than it arrived. No energy is created; it is borrowed from the heavy body's orbital motion, which slows by an amount too small to see. Voyager 2 reached Neptune on a chain of such assists. Use the launch preview to line one up.

Tidal tails

In 1972 Alar and Juri Toomre showed, with a few hundred test particles on a computer far weaker than your phone, that the bridges and tails of peculiar galaxies are tidal: a passing galaxy stretches the near and far sides of a disc in opposite directions, and differential rotation winds the debris into long arcs. Galaxy and intruder and Galaxy collision reproduce the recipe. Prograde encounters, where the discs spin the same way they orbit, throw the longest tails. Mergers are switched off in these arrangements because real stars almost never collide; the disc is collisionless and the stars pass straight through each other.

Mergers

When two bodies touch in the other arrangements they merge, conserving mass and momentum but not energy, since a real collision turns motion into heat. This is a crude stand-in for what an accretion disc does, but it captures the point: gravity is a ratchet. Large bodies grow larger, and a cloud of equals becomes a few heavy winners surrounded by leftovers. That is roughly how planets formed.

The honest caveats

  • Each body is a softened sphere, not a point, so the force stops growing inside its radius. This is what keeps close passes from producing infinities. The Pythagorean problem in particular depends on very close approaches, so the exact timing here will not match the textbook solution, though the qualitative story does.
  • Integration is velocity Verlet with an adaptive substep chosen from the strongest acceleration present. It conserves energy well but not perfectly. The energy drift readout subtracts the energy genuinely lost in mergers and carried away by ejected bodies, so what remains is integration error. Under 1% you are watching real physics; above 10% the picture is drifting. If the substep ceiling is hit during a violent pass, drift will climb.
  • Above about 400 bodies the force is computed with a Barnes-Hut tree, which groups distant bodies into single lumps. The force error is about one percent. The solver readout says which method is running.
  • Two dimensions only. Real discs are flattened anyway, but real galaxies also have dark matter halos and gas, neither of which is here. The rotation curves in the galaxy arrangements are Keplerian, not flat.

Things to try

  • Start Star and planets, click the comet, and watch its speed readout as it passes the star.
  • On Lagrange points, drop a body at rest right on L4 and another right on L1. One stays; one leaves.
  • Run Pythagorean three twice and compare the two runs. Then nudge the softening and run again.
  • On Galaxy and intruder, turn trails to zero and watch in the centre-of-mass view. The tail forms from the far side of the disc, not the near side.
  • Set gravity to 0.3 on Cold collapse. The cloud still falls in, slowly, and far fewer bodies merge.
  • Sling a heavy body through Binary pair and count how many of the debris orbits survive.
